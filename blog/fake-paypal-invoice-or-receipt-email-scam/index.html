Fake PayPal invoice or receipt email: what to do

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

An email arrives that looks like it came from PayPal. It says you have been sent an invoice for a few hundred dollars, or that a payment for a laptop, a crypto purchase, or a “subscription renewal” just went through. You did not buy anything. The message helpfully includes a phone number to call if the charge is a mistake. It is a very uncomfortable moment, because the email looks right, and in many cases it really did come from PayPal.

That is the part that makes this scam different, and it is why so many careful people get caught. Here is how it works, and what to do depending on how far it went.

Why this email can look completely real

In the most common version, the scammer opens a genuine PayPal account and uses PayPal’s own invoicing tool to send you a real invoice. The email comes from PayPal’s systems, so it often passes the checks that normally catch fake mail. The scammer types their message into the invoice note, which is where the “call this number about a billing problem” line comes from. A second version is a plain look-alike email with a fake receipt attached. Either way, the goal is rarely to get you to click “pay.” The goal is to get you on the phone.

What happens when you call

The person who answers sounds helpful and calm. They say they will cancel the charge, but first they need to “verify” your account. From there it usually moves one of a few ways: they ask you to install a remote-access program so they can “see the problem,” they walk you through logging in to your bank so a refund can be “processed,” or they claim they accidentally refunded too much and need you to send the difference back. This is the same playbook we describe in our posts on calling a fake customer service number and the Geek Squad renewal email. Only the opening is new.

If you have not called or paid

Then you are in good shape, and the main thing is to leave it alone. Do not call the number in the email, do not click through, and do not reply. Open the PayPal app or type paypal.com into your browser yourself and check whether any invoice or payment actually shows in your account. If nothing is there, there is no real charge. If a genuine invoice does appear, PayPal has options to report it as suspicious, and you can also forward the original email to PayPal’s phishing address, listed on their help pages. Unpaid invoices from strangers cost you nothing by sitting there.

If you called and gave access or details

Stop the call if it is still going, and disconnect from the internet if you let them install anything. Then work through it in this order. Call your bank or card issuer using the number on the back of your card, tell them what happened, and ask them to watch or freeze the account. Change the password on your PayPal account and on any email or banking login you used or typed during the call, from a device you trust. If you gave remote access, our guide on what to do if you gave a scammer remote access to your computer covers cleaning up the device itself. Keep the original email, the phone number, and any call log or screenshots, since those are what a report is built from.

If you sent money

Speed matters, so contact the bank, card issuer, or payment service you used right away and say it was fraud. If the money went through PayPal itself, report it to them from inside your account. For payments sent as friends and family, our post on sending money on PayPal to a scammer explains what is and is not recoverable. Then file a report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and at IC3.gov if you lost money. Reports are what regulators use to spot and shut down these accounts.

It is worth being realistic about one thing. Because the invoice came from a real PayPal account, PayPal can often close that account and sometimes freeze what is in it, but only if it hears about the invoice. That is one more reason to report, even if you never paid.

A habit that closes this door

Any time a message says there is a problem with an account and gives you a number to call, treat the number as part of the message you cannot trust. Look up the company’s contact details yourself, or just log in to your own account. A real billing problem will be waiting for you there. A fake one will not be.

If you called and are not sure what you gave away, you are welcome to text or email us and we will help you sort out what to check first.
