How to freeze your credit (it’s free and fast)

If you have ever had your information exposed in a breach, or found an account you did not open, someone has probably told you to “freeze your credit.” It sounds drastic, like locking down your whole financial life. It is not. A credit freeze is one of the simplest and most effective protections available, it costs nothing, and it takes about fifteen minutes. Here is what it does, how to set it up, and what to expect afterward.

What a credit freeze actually does

Lenders check your credit report before they approve a new loan, credit card, or account. A freeze tells the credit bureau to refuse to release your report to anyone who is opening something new. Because most lenders will not approve credit without seeing a report, a thief who has your name and Social Security number usually hits a wall. Your existing accounts keep working, your credit score is not affected, and you can still use your cards and pay your bills as usual. You can also still check your own report whenever you like, and employers or insurers you already work with are not locked out of the checks they are allowed to run.

Federal law has required the bureaus to offer freezes for free since 2018, so if anyone asks you to pay for one, that is a sign you are in the wrong place.

The three bureaus you need to contact

Each of the three major bureaus keeps its own file on you, and a freeze at one does not carry over to the others. To be protected, freeze all three: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Each lets you do it online, by phone, or by mail. Online is fastest. You will create a free account, confirm your identity with some personal details, and switch the freeze on. Save the PIN or login you set up, because you will need it later, and store it somewhere safe rather than in an email folder.

It is worth being a little careful about where you type all of this. Go to each bureau by typing its address yourself or using the links above rather than clicking a search ad or a link in a message, since look-alike sites do exist.

Lifting it when you need credit

A freeze stays in place until you remove it, so it does not expire on its own. When you want to apply for a mortgage, a car loan, or a new card, you can lift it temporarily or permanently, and the bureaus must act on an online or phone request within an hour. Most people lift it only at the bureau the lender will check, which you can ask the lender about, and then turn it back on afterward. Think of it as a door you open on purpose, not a wall that keeps you out.

Freeze versus fraud alert

You may also see the option of a fraud alert. An initial fraud alert is free, lasts one year, and asks lenders to take extra steps to verify it is really you before extending credit. If you have been a victim of identity theft and have a report on file, you can get an extended alert that lasts seven years. An alert is lighter-touch than a freeze, and a request to one bureau is passed along to the other two. If you have already had accounts opened in your name, a freeze is generally the stronger choice, and you can use both together. Our guide on what to do if someone opened accounts in your name walks through the full set of steps, including how to dispute what the thief opened.

The other places worth freezing

The big three are not the only files that matter. A few smaller reporting agencies feed information to lenders, landlords, and phone companies, and thieves sometimes slip through those. You can place freezes with Innovis, with the National Consumer Telecom and Utilities Exchange for phone and utility accounts, and with ChexSystems, which tracks bank account openings. None of these cost anything either. If you are working through a breach letter, our post on what to do when you get a data breach notification pairs well with this one.

Freezing for a child or a family member

Children can have a freeze too, and it is one of the best protections against a thief quietly using a young person’s clean record for years. Parents and guardians can request one by providing proof of identity and authority. The same goes for an adult you are legally responsible for. We cover the process and the warning signs in what to do about child identity theft.

If you have already seen fraud

A freeze stops new accounts. It does not undo anything that has already happened, so if you have spotted fraud, also review your reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com, and report the theft at IdentityTheft.gov, the FTC’s recovery site, which builds a personal recovery plan and the report you may need for disputes. Keep a simple record of what you reported, to whom, and when.

Taking a few minutes to freeze your credit is a quiet, practical step, and it puts you back in control of who gets to look at your file. If you would like a second set of eyes on a situation that feels bigger than a freeze can fix, you are welcome to reach out.
