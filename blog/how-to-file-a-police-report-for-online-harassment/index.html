How to file a police report for online harassment

You have the messages, the strange new accounts, maybe the fear that comes with checking your phone, and you have finally decided to call the police. Then the officer says it is just the internet, or that there is nothing they can do. That response is common, it is discouraging, and it does not mean your situation is not real or that you should stop. A clear, organized report makes it much harder to wave away.

Know what you are asking them to look at

Officers respond best to a specific behavior they recognize, so try to name it. Repeated unwanted contact after you asked it to stop, threats, posting your private information, impersonating you, sharing intimate images without consent, and monitoring your devices or location can all fall under harassment, stalking, or cyberstalking laws, and many states also have separate statutes for electronic harassment. The wording differs from place to place, and that is fine. You do not need to cite a law. Describe what happened, who you think is responsible if you know, and why it is frightening or harmful to you.

Build a one-page timeline first

A timeline is the single most useful thing you can hand over. List each incident in date order with the date, the time, the platform or phone number, and one plain sentence about what happened. Put the most serious items, such as threats or anything that mentions where you live or work, near the top of a short summary. Keep your feelings out of the log itself, since facts are easier for an officer to act on, and say how you felt in the conversation instead. If you are not sure where to start, our guide on preserving evidence in the first 24 hours walks through how to capture it safely.

Bring evidence in a form they can use

Print your screenshots and also bring them on a drive or send them by email, because some departments prefer one and some the other. Show the full screen, including the username, the date, and the web address. Save profile links and account IDs, not only display names, since names can be changed and IDs usually cannot. Do not delete the messages from your device, even the upsetting ones, and do not reply to the person to try to get them to confess. Both can complicate things later.

Ask for the report, not just a conversation

Calmly ask that an incident report be written and ask for the report number before you leave or hang up. A number gives you something to cite when you follow up, when you report to a platform, and if you ever need a protective order. If the officer declines to take a report, ask politely for the reason and for the name of a supervisor. You can also try the department’s detective unit or a victim advocate, which many counties and prosecutors’ offices have at no cost. If the contact crosses state lines or involves a child, you can also file at IC3.gov, the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center.

If they say no, you still have options

A declined report is frustrating, but it is not the end of the road. Platforms have their own reporting channels and can suspend accounts, and a police report number often speeds that up. Local victim services and domestic violence organizations help with online harassment even when no relationship is involved, and a civil protective order may be available without a criminal case. If the person keeps coming back under new names, this post on repeat account creation covers how to document that pattern. And if you were told nothing could be done on a scam or fraud report, what to do when no one responds may help too.

Look after your safety while you wait

If you ever feel you are in immediate danger, call 911 first and sort out the paperwork afterward. Otherwise, tighten your privacy settings, change passwords from a device you trust, and tell a friend or family member what is going on so you are not carrying it alone. You do not have to prove how upset you are to deserve help.
