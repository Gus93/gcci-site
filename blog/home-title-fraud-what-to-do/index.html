Home title fraud: what to do if your deed was changed

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

A letter arrives from a lender you have never heard of. Or a notice says your property taxes are overdue on a house you thought was paid off. Or you notice that a deed with your name on it was recorded at the county office, and you never signed it. If you are here because of something like that, take a breath. This is a frightening kind of fraud, but it is also a slow one, and that gives you time to act carefully.

What follows is how home title fraud works, what to check first, and the steps that matter most in the first few days.

How home title fraud works

Home title fraud, sometimes called deed fraud or deed theft, happens when someone records a forged document with the county that moves ownership of a property out of your name. The person behind it usually does not need to break into anything. Property records are public in most places, so a name, an address, and a parcel number are easy to find. From there they forge a signature, sometimes with a notary stamp that is fake or stolen, and file a deed transferring the home to themselves or to a shell company.

Once the deed is recorded, the fraudster may try to borrow against the equity, sell the property to an unsuspecting buyer, or rent it out. Vacant homes, second homes, rentals, and properties owned free and clear are common targets, as are homes owned by older people or by someone who has died. Often the owner finds out only when a lender, a tax bill, or a stranger at the door tells them something is wrong.

Signs worth checking

The warning signs are usually small. Mortgage or collection letters for loans you never took out. Mail about your property going to an address you do not recognize, or bills that suddenly stop arriving, which can mean someone changed your mailing address. A deed, lien, or refinance on file that you did not prepare. Unexplained credit inquiries. A tenant or a neighbor telling you someone has been showing the house.

None of these proves fraud on its own. Clerical errors and ordinary mail mix-ups happen. But any one of them is a good reason to look up your own property record today rather than wait.

Check the record first

Start with the office in your county that holds land records. It is usually called the recorder of deeds, county clerk, or register of deeds, and many counties offer a free online search by owner name or address. Look at the most recent documents recorded against your property. You are looking for any deed, mortgage, or lien you do not recognize, and for whether the grantor and signature look right. Print or save what you find and note the document numbers and recording dates.

Many counties also offer a free alert service that emails you when a document is recorded under your name or on your parcel. It is worth signing up for even if nothing is wrong yet, since it turns a problem you would discover months from now into one you hear about right away. Availability varies by county, so check your county’s website or call.

If a fraudulent deed is on file

Move quickly, and keep copies of everything. Call the county recorder and tell them you believe a forged document was recorded. They cannot always remove it themselves, but they can explain the process in your area and may flag the record. Contact a real estate attorney as soon as you can. Getting a forged deed set aside generally runs through the courts, and it is not something a form or a phone call to the county can fix on its own. If cost is a concern, your local bar association or LawHelp.org can point you to low-cost or pro bono help.

Notify your mortgage servicer, if you have one, and your title insurance company if you hold an owner’s policy, since either may open their own investigation. Then file a police report and keep the report number, because lenders and the county will often ask for it.

Treat it as identity theft too

Deed fraud almost always involves stolen personal information, so cover the rest of your identity at the same time. Report it at IdentityTheft.gov, which creates a recovery plan and an official report you can print. If money or an online element was involved, you can also file with IC3.gov. Place a freeze on your credit with all three bureaus; our guide on how to freeze your credit walks through it, and if new accounts have already appeared, see what to do if someone opened accounts in your name. Change passwords on your bank and email accounts, and confirm the mailing address on each is still yours.

Closing deals are a related risk

If you are in the middle of buying or selling a home and the money has not moved yet, a different fraud may be the one to watch for: a fake email telling you to wire funds to a new account. We cover that separately in real estate wire fraud: what to do now.

Beware the “title protection” pitch

After news stories about deed theft, some companies advertise monthly “home title lock” services, sometimes by mail or phone, using the fear of losing your house. Many of them do little that a free county alert and a periodic look at your own record do not already do. Be skeptical of anything that asks for payment upfront or claims it can “lock” your title. Read what is actually covered before you pay, and treat unsolicited calls the way you would any other pressure call.

A forged deed is stressful, but it is a recoverable problem, and people do get their homes back. If you would like a calm, private second set of eyes on what you are seeing, or help organizing the paperwork and timeline for your attorney, you are welcome to reach out. There is no judgment here.
