Unauthorized card charges: how to dispute them

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

You are scrolling through your bank app and there it is: a charge you don’t remember, maybe from a store you have never visited, maybe a small odd amount from a company you can’t place. It is an unsettling moment, but this is one of the most fixable kinds of fraud. Banks and card issuers handle it every day, and the law gives you real protection. What helps most is acting promptly and knowing which kind of card was used.

Start by calling the number on the back of your card

Contact your bank or card issuer using the number printed on the card or in your official banking app, not a number from a text, email, or search ad. Tell them which charges you don’t recognize and ask them to lock or replace the card. Replacing the card number stops further charges and also cuts off any saved subscriptions the thief set up. Ask for the dispute to be opened right then, and ask for a reference number. If you can, follow up in writing through the bank’s secure message center so there is a dated record.

Why debit and credit cards are treated differently

Credit cards carry the stronger protection. Under federal law your liability for unauthorized credit card charges is capped at $50, and most issuers waive even that. Debit cards are covered too, but the clock matters more because the money has already left your checking account. As the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains, reporting an unauthorized debit within two business days of learning about it generally limits your loss to $50. Wait longer and the cap can rise, and after about 60 days from the statement that first showed the charge, you can be left with much less protection. Many banks go beyond the minimum, but you should not count on it. This is general information, not legal advice, and your bank’s terms may add protection.

If the money is gone from your account and you are worried about rent or bills, say so. Ask when provisional credit will be applied while the bank investigates, since issuers often must resolve a dispute or restore funds within a set time.

Sort out whether it is truly unauthorized

Before you finish the dispute, take a calm second look. Some charges look strange but are legitimate: a subscription billed under the parent company’s name, a hotel or rental hold, a trial that rolled into a paid plan, or a family member using the card. Searching the exact merchant text on your statement often clears it up. Genuine fraud tends to look different. It might be a run of small test charges, a purchase in another state or country, or a payment through a service you don’t use. Report the real ones, and for the ones you recognize but didn’t want, contact the merchant first or tell the bank it is a billing dispute rather than fraud.

Find out how it happened

Card numbers are stolen in many ways: a data breach, a skimmer at a gas pump or ATM, a fake checkout page, or a phishing message. Think back over recent purchases. If you entered your card on a site you now doubt, our guide to the fake online store scam covers what to check. If you clicked a link in a message, see what to do after clicking a phishing link. If a notice arrived saying your information was exposed, this post on breach notification letters explains the next steps.

Also check your email and phone. If the charges came with password-reset messages you didn’t request, someone may have more than your card number, and you will want to secure your accounts as well.

Tighten things up afterward

Turn on transaction alerts in your banking app so every charge pings your phone. Review the next two or three statements line by line. Update the card on any legitimate subscriptions you want to keep. If the thief had personal details beyond the card, consider a free credit freeze, and our guide on how to freeze your credit walks through it. Keep screenshots of the charges and your dispute reference number in one place.

Report it

Reporting is optional for a small, quickly reversed charge, but it helps if the problem is larger or part of a pattern. You can file with ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and a police report may be requested by your bank for bigger losses. If someone talked you into authorizing a payment yourself, the rules can differ, and you should tell the bank exactly what happened rather than only that it was “unauthorized.”

Seeing a charge you didn’t make is not a failure on your part. Quick action, a replaced card, and a documented dispute resolve most of these cases. If it turns out to be part of something bigger, you are welcome to reach out to us privately and we can talk it through.
