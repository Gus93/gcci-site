Embarrassed after being scammed? What to do next

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

If you are reading this because you were scammed and you feel foolish, please start here: the people who run these schemes do this for a living. They rehearse scripts, buy stolen data, copy real logos, and study what makes a person trust a voice or a message. Being targeted by a professional operation is not a character flaw. Smart, careful, experienced people lose money to scams every year, and the embarrassment you feel is one of the main things scammers count on.

Why the shame matters more than it seems

Shame is not just an unpleasant feeling. It changes what people do next. Victims who are embarrassed often wait to tell their bank, skip reporting, or keep quiet with family, and every day of silence makes recovery harder. Embarrassment also makes people easy targets for a second round. A scammer who knows you are ashamed can offer a way to “fix it quietly.” Treating this as something that happened to you, rather than something you did, is the first practical step.

Take the time-sensitive steps first

Before you spend any energy on how it happened, do the things that depend on speed. Call your bank or card issuer using the number on your card and tell them exactly what occurred, including that you sent the payment yourself if that is the case. Bank staff hear these stories constantly and they are not judging you. Change passwords on any account the scammer touched, starting with email. Save the messages, receipts, phone numbers, and wallet addresses before they disappear, and our post on preserving evidence in the first 24 hours covers how.

Tell one person you trust

You do not need to announce this to everyone. Choose one person who will respond with support rather than a lecture, a friend, a sibling, a spouse, a coworker you trust. Say it plainly: “I was scammed and I need a second pair of eyes.” Carrying it alone tends to make it heavier, and a second person can spot details you are too close to see. If someone you love is the one who was scammed, our guide on helping a family member who is being scammed offers wording that keeps the conversation kind.

Watch for the second scam

People who have been scammed are often contacted again, sometimes within days. The new message may claim to be a government agent, a lawyer, or a recovery company that can get your money back for an upfront fee. These are frequently the same operation or one that bought a list of victims. A legitimate refund comes through your bank, card issuer, or a regulator, and it does not require a fee to someone who contacted you first. Before paying anyone, read how to tell whether a recovery service is legitimate.

Report it, even if it feels pointless

Reporting is not about shame; it is about adding your case to the pattern investigators use to connect incidents. You can file with ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, where money moved online, with IC3.gov. It is normal to hear nothing back, and if that happens, here is what to do next. A report still creates a record you can point to later with your bank or an attorney.

Give yourself room to recover

Even when the money is small, being deceived can leave you second-guessing your own judgment. That reaction is common, and it usually eases with time and with talking about it. If the stress is affecting your sleep or daily life, speaking with a counselor or a victim support line is a reasonable step, and there is no minimum amount of loss required to deserve support. Meanwhile, rebuild trust in yourself through small, concrete habits: slow down on anything urgent, verify through a second channel, and never feel rude hanging up.

You are not the first person this has happened to, and you will not be the last. If you would like to talk through what happened, privately and without judgment, you are welcome to reach out to us.
