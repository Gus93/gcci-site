That Medicare call during open enrollment: what to do

Your phone rings, and the voice on the other end is friendly and well-rehearsed. They are calling about your Medicare coverage. There is a new card, a new benefit, or a deadline you are about to miss, and all they need is your Medicare number to “verify” you. If this happened to you this week, you are in very large company. Open enrollment runs from October 15 to December 7 each year, and scammers treat it like a holiday shopping season.

The first thing to know is that this is not your fault, and it is rarely about being gullible. The callers are good at sounding like the system you already depend on, and they call when you are expecting to hear about plan choices. Let’s go through how to tell what is real, and what to do if you already said too much.

What the real Medicare does and doesn’t do

Medicare does not call you out of the blue to sell you something or to ask for your Medicare number, Social Security number, or bank details. You do not need to pay for a new Medicare card; your card is free, and nobody has to “activate” it. Medicare also does not threaten to cancel your coverage unless you act on the phone today. If a call includes pressure, secrecy, or a request for a payment by gift card, wire, or cryptocurrency, that alone tells you what it is.

Legitimate insurance agents and plan sellers exist, and open enrollment is a real period when you may be contacted if you gave permission or asked for information. The difference is that a real one can wait while you hang up and check. A scammer cannot, which is why the urgency is the tell.

The common scripts

Most calls fall into a few patterns. One offers a “free” back brace, genetic test, or other equipment, with the goal of getting your Medicare number to bill for things you never receive. Another claims your card is expiring or that a new one with a chip is being mailed, and asks you to confirm your number. A third promises extra benefits, lower premiums, or a rebate if you switch plans today. Some callers already know your name, your town, or part of your number, which feels reassuring but usually comes from old data breaches or purchased lists, not from any real relationship with you.

If you are not sure, hang up and call back

You never need to be polite to a call that makes you uneasy. Hang up, then call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) yourself, or look up the number for your plan on the back of your card. If you want help comparing plans from someone with no product to sell, every state has a free counseling program called SHIP, which you can find through Medicare.gov. Doing the check yourself, on a number you chose, is the single most reliable habit.

If you already gave out your Medicare number

Take a breath; this is fixable. Call 1-800-MEDICARE, tell them what you shared and when, and ask what they can do to protect your account, including whether a new number is appropriate. Then watch your Medicare Summary Notices and your account at Medicare.gov for claims you don’t recognize. Read every explanation of benefits when it arrives for the next year or so, because misuse tends to show up weeks or months later. If claims do appear for care you never had, our guide to medical identity theft walks through correcting your records step by step.

If you also gave a Social Security number, a bank account, or a card number, the stakes are a little higher. Contact your bank right away, and consider a free credit freeze; our guide to freezing your credit takes about ten minutes. If money has already left your account, speed matters, and what to do if you wired money to a scammer explains who to call first.

Report it, even if nothing was lost

Reports help investigators see patterns even when no one gets money back. You can report the call to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and Medicare fraud specifically to 1-800-MEDICARE or the HHS Office of Inspector General. Write down the phone number that called, the date, and what they asked for while it is fresh.

Most of these calls end with a hang-up and nothing worse. If something about yours doesn’t sit right, or you can’t tell what was taken, you are welcome to reach out and we can look at it together, privately.
