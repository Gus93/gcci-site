USPS change of address fraud: what to do next

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

Your mail has gone quiet, or a letter arrives from the post office confirming a change of address you never requested. Maybe a bill did not show up, a bank statement is late, or a neighbor mentions they received an envelope with your name on it. Any of these can mean someone has filed a change-of-address request in your name, which sends your mail to a place they control. It is a quieter kind of identity theft than most, and the good news is that it can usually be stopped quickly once you spot it.

We will walk through how it works, what to do in the first day, and how to keep it from happening again.

How change-of-address fraud works

Anyone can submit a change-of-address request to the U.S. Postal Service online or at a counter, and the thief only needs your name and your address, both easy to find. Once the forwarding is active, your mail starts going to them: pre-approved credit offers, bank and card statements, tax documents, replacement cards, and anything else with account numbers on it. They use what they learn to open accounts, take over existing ones, or intercept new cards before you ever see them.

USPS sends a confirmation notice to both your old and new addresses when a change is filed, so the letter you did not expect is often the first real warning. It is easy to toss it as junk, so if one arrives, treat it as important. The other common signs are a sudden drop in the amount of mail you receive, a missed bill that you know should have come, or a company telling you it mailed something you never got.

What to do in the first day

Contact your local post office in person or by phone and tell them you did not file the change of address, and ask that the forwarding be cancelled and your mail held for pickup in the meantime. Then report it to the U.S. Postal Inspection Service at uspis.gov/report, since mail fraud is their jurisdiction. It also helps to sign up for Informed Delivery on usps.com, which emails you a preview of the mail headed to your address, so you can see what is going where. If someone has already signed you up, the account may show you mail you recognize, and that tells you what has been exposed.

Next, think about what was probably in the mail you missed. Call the banks and card issuers whose statements went missing, tell them your mail may have been diverted, and ask them to watch for changes to your address or contact details. Pull your credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and look for accounts you do not recognize. A credit freeze is free, takes a few minutes with each bureau, and stops new accounts from being opened in your name, so we would do it today. Our guide on how to freeze your credit goes through each step.

Report it and keep a record

File a report at IdentityTheft.gov, which gives you a recovery plan and a report you can use with banks and creditors. Keep copies of the USPS confirmation letter, the date you called the post office, the name of the person you spoke to, and any report numbers. If accounts were opened or money moved, our guide on what to do if someone opened accounts in your name covers the next steps, and a local police report can be useful for some creditors.

Watch for what comes next

For the next several months, check your accounts more often than usual, and keep an eye on your mail for letters about accounts you never opened. If a check was mailed to you and never arrived, see what to do if your mailed check was stolen. Also be careful of anyone who contacts you offering to “fix” the problem for a fee. The post office, your bank, and the credit bureaus do not charge to cancel a forwarding order, freeze your file, or correct fraud.

Finding out your mail has been redirected is unsettling, but it is a problem with a clear set of steps, and most of them take less than an hour. Acting early, while the thief has had little time with your mail, is what makes the biggest difference.
