What to do if you paid a deposit on a fake rental listing

The place looked right, the price was a little better than everything else, and the landlord was easy to reach until the moment your deposit landed. Now the number goes to voicemail, the listing is gone, or someone else answers the door at the address. If that is where you are, this guide is for what to do next, in order.

First, the part worth hearing before the practical steps. Rental listing scams work because housing searches are stressful and rushed by design. The scammer copied photos from a real listing, priced it to make you move fast, and invented a reason they could not show the place in person. People of every age and background pay deposits on fake rentals every day. This is not about you being careless.

Stop paying anything else

If the “landlord” is still in contact, the requests will keep coming: a second month up front, a key-shipping fee, a background check charge, a refundable pet deposit. None of it unlocks the apartment, because there is no apartment waiting for you. Stop sending money, but do not announce that you know it is a scam or delete the conversation. You want the thread intact and the scammer unhurried.

Try to pull the payment back today

What is possible depends entirely on how you paid, and the clock matters more than anything else on this list.

  • Zelle, Venmo, or Cash App: contact the app and your bank now, report the payment as fraud, and ask what recall options exist. We cover the details for Zelle, Venmo, and Cash App separately.
  • Wire transfer: call your bank and ask for a recall attempt immediately. The first hours matter; our guide on wired money walks through it.
  • Credit or debit card: call the number on the back of the card and dispute the charge as fraud. Cards give you the strongest reversal rights of any method here.
  • Crypto or gift cards: the payment itself will not reverse, but report it anyway. Tracing is sometimes possible, and the report builds the record that makes anything else doable.

Save the evidence before the listing disappears

Fake listings vanish quickly once a victim starts asking questions, so capture everything now: the listing itself if it is still up, the photos, the address, every message and email, the phone number and email address you dealt with, the payment confirmation, and any lease or application documents they sent you. Screenshots are fine; keep them somewhere you will not lose them. Our guide to preserving evidence in the first 24 hours covers how to do this cleanly.

Report it in the right places

File a report with IC3.gov and ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and report the listing to the platform where you found it so it comes down before the next renter pays. Ask your local police department for a report and a case number, especially if the loss is significant; banks and platforms move faster when there is an official reference attached. If you handed over a Social Security number or photo ID on a fake rental application, also visit IdentityTheft.gov and follow the recovery plan there, because that is an identity exposure on top of the lost deposit.

Watch for the second scam

Once your contact information circulates on a victim list, expect messages from “recovery agents” who claim they can get your deposit back for an upfront fee, and sometimes a fake follow-up from the “property manager” offering a refund if you cover a small processing charge first. Both are the same crew, or their customers. Nobody legitimate charges an upfront fee to recover scam losses, and refunds do not require you to pay anything. If you already reported it and heard nothing back, here is what silence actually means and what to do next.

If the loss is large

Most rental deposit losses are painful but modest, and the steps above are the right-sized response. If you lost several months of rent, moving costs, or fees on top of the deposit, a documented investigation can sometimes identify where the money went and who received it. Our Investigation Help page explains what we take on, and for larger cases that need court-grade attribution we point people to Rexxfield.

The deposit may or may not come back, and an honest guide has to say so. What you control today is whether the payment gets challenged while it can still be challenged, whether the evidence survives, and whether the listing comes down before it catches the next person. That is worth the hour it takes.
