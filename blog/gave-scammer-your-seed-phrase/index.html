What to do if you gave a scammer your seed phrase

Maybe a fake support agent walked you through it. Maybe a site asked you to “validate” your wallet and you pasted the twelve words in. However it happened, a scammer now has your seed phrase, and that is a different kind of emergency than a stolen password. Let’s take it calmly, in order.

Move anything that’s left, right now

If there is still crypto in that wallet, assume the clock is running. A seed phrase hands whoever holds it complete control, so the wallet is no longer yours to defend. Set up a brand new wallet on a device you trust, ideally one that never touched the scam, and move any remaining funds there. Don’t reuse the old phrase anywhere. If you own a hardware wallet, generate a fresh set of words on it rather than restoring the compromised ones.

Why you can’t just change the password

A seed phrase isn’t a login. It is the master key to the wallet itself, the thing that can regenerate every address and private key inside it. There is no reset button, no support line that can revoke it, no way to lock the wallet while keeping the same phrase. That part is hard to hear, and it’s exactly what the scammer is counting on you not knowing. The only real fix is to walk away from the exposed wallet and start clean.

What the scammer can and can’t reach

They can drain any asset sitting in that wallet, and a lot of them automate it, so the transfer fires within minutes of the words landing. What they cannot touch is anything that never lived in that wallet. Your exchange account sits behind its own login and 2FA. Your bank is separate. Other wallets with different phrases are untouched. Secure those next. Change exchange passwords, reset two-factor authentication, and check that nobody quietly added a new withdrawal address while you weren’t looking.

Can the stolen crypto be traced?

Yes, and this is where a clear head pays off. Every move out of your wallet is written to the blockchain, so an investigator can follow the funds across addresses and often to the exchange where the thief tries to cash out. That trail won’t reverse the transaction, because crypto doesn’t work that way. What it gives you is a documented record of where the money went, which is the thing a police report, an exchange fraud team, or a civil claim can actually act on. Reporting fast matters here. Funds that land at a compliant exchange can sometimes be flagged before they scatter through mixers.

The next hour

Write everything down while it’s fresh: the wallet address, the transaction hashes, the site or person that asked for your phrase, any usernames or links. File at IC3.gov and report to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If a known exchange received the funds, contact their support and hand over the transaction details. And brace for the follow-up con. Within a few days, “recovery experts” tend to surface in your DMs promising to claw it all back for a fee. Real tracing never works on an upfront guarantee like that, so treat anyone who promises recovery as the second scam.

Where to put your energy

If the loss is large enough that you want a documented trail for a civil case or an insurance question, our Investigation Help page covers the smaller-case work we sometimes take on. For five- and six-figure losses that need to hold up in court, we route to Rexxfield for court-grade fund tracing. Either way, nobody legitimate will ask you to pay in crypto to unlock your own money.

You trusted the wrong person for a moment. Scammers engineer that moment for a living, so it isn’t a verdict on you. Move quickly, keep a record of everything, and let the chain do the part it’s good at.
