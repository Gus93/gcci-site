Cash flip scam: what to do if you sent money

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

Someone online told you that you could send them a small amount of money and get a much larger amount back, maybe $50 turned into $500, maybe $200 into $2,000. It may have come from a friend’s account, a stranger in your messages, or a post promising “flips” for anyone who asks. You sent the money, and now they have gone quiet, blocked you, or asked for one more payment before the big one arrives. That is a cash flip scam, sometimes called a money flip, and the first thing to know is that it is a common one, and that many thoughtful people have been caught by it.

We will walk through how it works, what is realistic to do about the money, and how to protect the accounts you used.

How a cash flip scam works

The pitch is simple: a person claims to have a way to multiply cash, using a payment app, a bank “glitch,” or a connection they will not explain. To prove it, they post screenshots of people who supposedly received big payouts, and those screenshots are easy to fake. Some scammers go further and send you a small payment first, which feels like evidence that the system is real. Then they ask for the larger amount, and the payout never comes.

When the flip does not arrive, the story changes. There is a “fee” to release the money, a “verification” payment, or a problem that only another transfer can fix. Each extra payment is bait for the next one. Another version asks for your login or a security code so they can “process” the flip on your account, which can lead to something worse than the original loss, because your account may then be used to move other people’s stolen money.

What to do right away

Stop sending anything, including the small final payment that would supposedly unlock everything. Then screenshot the conversation, the scammer’s profile and username, and every payment confirmation, before they delete their account. Payment apps usually have an in-app way to report a payment as fraud, and you should use it today, along with a call to the bank or card that funds the app. If you shared a password or a code, change that password immediately and turn on two-step verification, and check whether any new devices or linked accounts appeared. If you were asked to log in for them or you saw payments you do not recognize, see our guide on what to do if a scammer used you as a money mule.

Report it

File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, if there is any ongoing pattern or larger loss, with IC3.gov. Report the profile to the platform where the offer appeared too, since removed accounts are one of the few things that actually slow these scams down. The app-specific steps differ slightly, so our guides for Cash App, Zelle, and Venmo go through each one.

Be realistic about getting it back

Peer-to-peer apps treat payments you sent yourself as authorized, which is why banks and apps are often reluctant to reverse them, even when you were clearly deceived. Reporting quickly gives you the best chance, and it is worth asking, but we would rather tell you plainly that many people do not get the money back.

Be careful about what comes next. After a loss, victims sometimes see messages or videos claiming that a government agency is handing out payouts to people scammed on payment apps, usually for a fee or a login. Consumer Reports has warned about exactly this, and it is a second scam. Recovery offers that need money first or access to your accounts are never a rescue.

How to avoid it next time

No legitimate system turns a small payment into a large one, and the app companies say so directly. If an offer depends on you sending money first, on secrecy, or on someone else’s urgency, that is the whole tell. Treat payment apps like cash handed to a stranger, and only send money to people you know and have confirmed in a second way. If a friend suddenly offers a flip, their account may have been taken over, so call them before you reply.

Sending that money was a hopeful decision, not a foolish one, because the scam is built to look like a chance. Documenting what happened and reporting it quickly is the best use of the next hour.
