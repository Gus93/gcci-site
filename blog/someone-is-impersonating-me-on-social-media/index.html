What to do if someone is impersonating you on social media

A friend sends you a screenshot: there is a second account with your name, your photo, maybe a handle one character off from yours. It is following the people you know and sending them messages as if it were you. Perhaps it is asking your contacts for money, pushing a crypto “opportunity,” or just posting things you never said. Seeing yourself copied like this is unsettling, and the first feeling is usually a mix of anger and helplessness.

The good news is that a fake profile is one of the more fixable problems in this space. You did not do anything wrong by having a public photo, and there are clear, effective steps you can take today. Here is where to start.

Capture the evidence before it disappears

Before you report anything, take a minute to save proof. Impersonation accounts often vanish the moment they are reported or once the person behind them realizes they have been spotted, and you will want a record either way. Screenshot the fake profile in full, including the username, the display name, the bio, the profile photo, and the exact URL in the address bar. If it has messaged anyone, ask them to screenshot those conversations too, with dates visible. Our guide on preserving evidence in the first 24 hours walks through how to capture this cleanly so it holds up if you ever need it for the platform, your bank, or the police.

Report the fake account to the platform

Every major platform has a dedicated impersonation report, and this is the fastest route to getting the account removed. On Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, X, and LinkedIn, look for the report option on the profile itself and choose the “pretending to be someone” or “impersonation” category rather than generic spam, since that flag routes the case to the right team. Report it from your own real account so the platform can see the genuine profile alongside the fake one. If the impersonator copied a specific photo or post of yours, mention that, because a clear side-by-side makes the decision easy for the reviewer. You can usually submit a report even if you do not have an account on that platform yourself, using its impersonation web form.

Tell the people it is aimed at

The account exists to fool the people who trust you, so let them know before it does. A short post from your real profile saying “there is a fake account using my name, please do not respond to it and report it if you can” does two things at once: it protects your contacts and it gives the platform more reports to act on. Ask a few friends to report the fake profile as well. If it has already messaged people asking for money or gift cards, tell them plainly not to pay and to treat any such request as a scam no matter how much it sounds like you.

Lock down your own accounts

An impersonator does not need to break into your account to copy your public photos, but it is worth making sure they have not. Change your password to something long and unique, turn on two-factor authentication, and review recent logins and connected apps for anything you do not recognize. Consider setting your existing profile and photos to a tighter privacy level for a while, so there is less material for anyone to lift. This is also a sensible moment to think about your wider identity: if the impersonation is part of something larger, our guide on what to do when someone opens accounts in your name covers freezing your credit and catching misuse early.

Report it beyond the platform

Platform removal handles the immediate account, but a report on the record matters too, especially if the impersonator used your identity to defraud people. The FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov is the place to log it; imposter scams remain the single most reported category, with people reporting billions in losses to them last year, and each report helps regulators see the patterns. If anyone was defrauded, or if you were, add a report at IC3.gov and file with your local police, which also gives you a report number you can reference with banks or the platform. Keep those confirmations with your evidence file.

If your photos are being used to harm you

Sometimes impersonation shades into something heavier, like a profile built to harass you or one using intimate images. If private photos are involved, do not engage with any demands, and see our guide on what to do when someone threatens to share your photos, which covers StopNCII and the reporting tools built for exactly this. And if you find yourself wanting to know who is actually behind the account, our piece on how to find a scammer’s identity is honest about what you can uncover on your own and where a real name tends to live.

A fake version of you online feels personal, because it is. But it is also a thin, brittle thing that platforms take down every day. Save the proof, report it through the right channel, tell your people, and give it a little time. The copy does not get to keep your name.
