Someone is posting lies about you online

Someone is posting lies about you online, and the account doing it has no real name attached. Maybe it is a review claiming you stole money, a blog or complaint-site page built around your name, a thread accusing you of something you did not do, or a handle that appears every time your name comes up. It may be one post or it may be a steady drip. Either way, it is being read by people whose opinion matters to you, and there is no obvious person to talk to about it.

That combination, real damage from an unreal source, is what makes this so hard to sit with. But this is a well-worn path, and there is a sequence to it that works better than reacting in the moment. Here is where to start.

Don’t answer it yet

The strongest instinct is to reply, correct the record, or demand a retraction, and it is almost always worth waiting on. A public reply tells the algorithm the post is interesting and lifts it into more feeds. It also gives the person exactly the reaction they were fishing for, which in most cases is the point of the exercise. If they are anonymous, engaging also risks tipping them off that you are documenting, and accounts that feel watched tend to delete things you have not saved yet.

Silence in public does not mean doing nothing. It means the work happens somewhere the other person cannot see it.

Save everything before you do anything else

Assume the posts will disappear. Take full-page screenshots that show the visible date, the account name, and the URL in the address bar, and save the URL of each post as text you can click later, not just as a picture. Note the time and your timezone. If it is a video or a live stream, save a copy of the file rather than trusting the link. Keep it all in one folder with a simple running log of what appeared when, because a pattern over weeks is far more persuasive to a platform, a lawyer, or a court than any single screenshot. Our guide on preserving evidence in the first 24 hours covers how to capture this so it holds up.

Do not edit, crop, or annotate the originals. Keep those untouched and make copies if you need to mark things up.

What actually counts as defamation

This matters, because it determines what you can do next. Defamation generally means a false statement of fact, published to others, that damages your reputation. The words “of fact” do most of the work. “She stole $4,000 from the client account” is a factual claim that is either true or false. “She is the worst manager I have ever had” is opinion, and opinion is very hard to touch legally no matter how unfair it feels. Reading through your saved posts and separating the checkable factual claims from the insults will tell you quickly whether you have a defamation problem, a harassment problem, or both.

It is also worth knowing that the platform hosting the post is usually not legally responsible for it in the United States, which is why “this is defamation, take it down” sent to a host often goes nowhere on its own. The person who wrote it is responsible. That is why identifying them tends to be the hinge the whole thing turns on.

Report it to the platform on the right grounds

Platforms rarely act on defamation, but they act reliably on the things their own rules already forbid: harassment campaigns, targeted abuse, impersonation, revealing private information, coordinated inauthentic behaviour, and fake reviews from someone who was never a customer. Read the actual policy, find the clause your situation fits, and report against that clause rather than making a general complaint. If several posts are part of one campaign, report them together and say so, because a documented pattern of targeting gets a different level of review than a lone post.

If the posts include your address, phone number, or workplace, treat that as its own emergency and handle it first. Our guide on what to do if someone doxxed you walks through those removals, which usually move faster than anything else.

What search engines can and cannot remove

Search results are a separate lever from the post itself. Google will not decide a defamation dispute for you, but it does accept legal removal requests through its legal help portal for content that is unlawful under your local law, and it will act on a court order that finds specific content defamatory once you submit that order to it directly. Removing something from search does not delete the page, but it changes who ever sees it, and for most people the search result is the real injury. If the page also carries your personal details, Google’s “results about you” process is a faster route than any legal one.

Finding out who is behind the account

Anonymous is not the same as unidentifiable. Most people running a campaign against someone they know leave a trail: an image reused from another profile, a writing habit, a timezone in their posting hours, a detail only a handful of people knew, a domain registration, an email address recycled from an old account. Some of that you can piece together yourself, and our post on how to find a scammer’s identity covers the open-source side and where it stops.

Beyond that point, the identifying records sit with the platform, the host, or the ISP, and only legal process pulls them out. In practice that means a lawyer filing what is often called a John Doe action, getting the court’s permission, and subpoenaing the provider for registration and login records. It is not fast and it is not free, typically several weeks and a few thousand dollars, and it works best when the statements are clearly factual rather than opinion and when you move before the provider’s logs age out. That last part is the reason the evidence-saving step comes first and the reason waiting rarely helps.

Where an independent investigation earns its keep is in the middle: establishing which accounts belong to the same person, tying them to a plausible identity, and assembling the factual record so that a lawyer’s subpoena asks the right provider for the right thing. That work often makes the difference between a legal step that lands and one that comes back empty.

Look after the rest of your life while this runs

Two practical things help. Tell the people whose opinion actually matters, your employer, your key clients, your family, before they find it themselves; a short, calm heads-up from you removes almost all of the post’s power. And set up a search alert on your own name so you learn about new posts from a notification rather than from repeatedly searching yourself, which is its own kind of harm and tends to fill entire evenings.

None of this resolves overnight. But documented, reported, and traced beats argued-with, and the quiet version of this process is the one that tends to end with the posts gone and a name attached.
