Someone built a fake website copying your business

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

A customer writes to say thanks for the great deal, and you have no idea what deal they mean. Or someone sends you a link to a website with your logo, your photos, and your business name on it, but you did not build it. The prices are too good, the checkout is strange, and your real customers are the ones paying for it. It is a gut-punch, especially if you have spent years building a reputation, and it is more common than most owners expect.

What you are looking at is a cloned or lookalike website. Copying a site takes a scammer very little effort, since your public pages can be saved and re-uploaded in minutes under a domain that is one letter off from yours. The goal is usually to collect payments for goods that will never ship, or to steal logins and card numbers. It is not a sign that your own site was hacked, and it is not your fault. What matters now is acting in a sensible order.

First, confirm your own site is fine

Spend a few minutes checking that your real website, hosting account, and domain login are untouched. Make sure your own domain is not about to expire, change the password on your domain registrar account if you are unsure, and turn on two-step verification there. This is usually a quick check, and it lets you tell customers with confidence that the problem is a copy, not your real site.

Capture the fake site before it disappears

Takedowns work faster, and any later report is stronger, when you have a record. Save the full web address, take screenshots of the pages with the date visible, and note any payment details, email addresses, phone numbers, or social accounts listed on it. Please do not enter any real information or place a test order. Our guide to preserving evidence in the first 24 hours covers how to keep this in a form that holds up if you ever need it.

Report it to the places that can switch it off

No single office removes a fake site, so you generally report it in a few places at once. The most effective are usually the hosting provider and the domain registrar. You can look up who they are by entering the fake domain into a lookup tool such as the one at ICANN, then use the “abuse” contact or form each company publishes. Explain plainly that the site impersonates your business, include your screenshots, and point to your real site as proof of ownership. If the fake site uses your logo or product photos, a copyright notice to the host can also be effective, because hosts have a formal process for those.

It also helps to report the site to browser warning services, so visitors see a red warning page instead of the fake shop. Google Safe Browsing and Microsoft both take reports of deceptive sites, and a warning often reduces the damage within a day or two even before the site comes down. If the scammer is advertising the fake site on social media or in search ads, report the ads and the pages directly to the platform too.

Tell your customers, calmly

A short, factual notice on your real website and social pages does a lot. Say that a copy of your site exists, give your real web address, and explain how you take payment, so people can spot the difference. Customers who already paid the fake site should contact their card issuer or payment app right away, and our post on what to do if you ordered from a fake online store walks them through it. Keep the tone steady and avoid naming a suspect, since you rarely know who is behind it.

File official reports

Report the site to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI’s IC3, especially if customers lost money. These reports will probably not lead to a quick arrest, but they build a record, and they help when a host or registrar asks whether you have reported it. If your trademark is registered, tell the companies that, because it can strengthen your case. Bigger disputes over a lookalike domain can go through a formal domain-dispute process or a lawyer, and that is a decision worth making with legal advice, not something to rush.

Expect some whack-a-mole

Be realistic here. Scammers often move to a new domain after the first is removed, and finding the person behind it is difficult because registrations are cheap and often private. A good habit is to keep a short list of every fake address you find and report each one. If scammers also leave fake reviews or copy your social pages, our posts on fake negative reviews on your business and someone impersonating you on social media cover those pieces.

If the clones keep coming, or customers have been hurt and you are not sure what to do next, text or email us and we can talk it through privately.
