What to do if you gave money to a fake charity

A post, a text, or a call made a cause feel urgent, and you gave without stopping to check. Maybe it was disaster relief after a storm on the news, a sick child's medical fund, or a veterans' group with a name that sounded familiar. Now the receipt looks thin, nobody answers the number back, or a friend mentioned the same “charity” called them too. That instinct to help is not the problem. Scammers are counting on it, and they build their pitches to skip past the questions you would normally ask.

You are not the first person this has happened to, and pressing pause now to sort out what happened is a reasonable, useful thing to do. Here is where to start.

The signs it wasn’t a real charity

A few patterns show up again and again in charity scams. The ask arrived right after a disaster, tragedy, or news story, timed for when people are moved and not thinking about paperwork. The name was close to a well-known organization’s, off by a word or two. You were pushed to act immediately, sometimes with a countdown or a claim that matching funds were about to expire. And the payment method was one that is hard to reverse or trace, like a gift card, wire transfer, cash app, or cryptocurrency, rather than a credit card or check to a named, registered organization.

None of this means you were careless. These campaigns are built by people who run them full-time and test what gets a response.

Check whether the charity is even real

Before anything else, look up the organization independently, not through any link or number the fundraiser gave you. Search its name plus “charity navigator,” “BBB Wise Giving Alliance,” or “give.org,” and check whether your state’s Attorney General or Secretary of State lists it as a registered charitable organization. A legitimate nonprofit will have a public tax filing, a real address, and a track record you can verify in minutes. If none of that turns up, or the group only existed as a social media page created a week ago, you are very likely dealing with a fake.

Try to get the payment back

What is recoverable depends entirely on how you paid. If you used a credit card, call the card issuer, explain that the recipient may be a fraudulent charity, and ask about disputing the charge. If you paid by debit card, wire, gift card, or a peer-to-peer app, act fast, since those methods are much harder to reverse the longer you wait. Our guides on what to do if you sent gift cards, wired money to a scammer, and sent money on Cash App walk through the specific steps for each payment method. If the donation went out as crypto, our post on reporting a crypto scam and what can still be traced covers what is realistic to expect.

Report it, even for a small amount

File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, which tracks charity fraud specifically and shares data with state regulators who can act on patterns across many small reports. If the solicitation came through a website, social media ad, or crowdfunding page, report it to that platform directly, since most have a dedicated fraud or fake-fundraiser reporting flow. If you gave through cryptocurrency, Chainabuse.com lets you flag the receiving wallet address so it is on record if others report the same one. And if the ask came in over the phone or through a scam website, IC3.gov is the right place for that report too.

Watch for the follow-up

Once you have reported a fake charity or disputed a payment, be cautious of anyone who reaches out afterward offering to help you get the money back for an upfront fee. That is a second scam built on the first one, and it is common enough that we cover it in our piece on how to tell a real recovery process from a recovery scam. Legitimate refunds come from your bank, your card issuer, or a regulator, never from a stranger who contacts you first and asks to be paid before they start.

Giving because something moved you is not a mistake. The people running these campaigns are betting that you will not check, so checking, reporting, and telling the people around you what you found is exactly the right response.
