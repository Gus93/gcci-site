Amazon account hacked? What to do about strange orders

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

Maybe it was an email confirming an order for a gaming console you never looked at. Maybe your password suddenly stopped working, or you opened your order history and found a string of gift card purchases shipped to an address three states away. However you found out, a hacked Amazon account feels personal. It holds your address, your payment cards, your shopping habits, and sometimes years of household history.

The good news is that this is one of the more fixable kinds of account takeover. Amazon has a dedicated process for unauthorized orders, and charges made on a hijacked account are usually refundable when you report them promptly. Here is how to work through it calmly, in order.

First, make sure the warning is real

Before you click anything, separate a real problem from a fake alert. Some of the most common scams this time of year are emails, texts, and phone calls claiming your Amazon account has been hacked, designed to scare you into handing over a login or a one-time code. We cover that pattern in our guide to the fake Amazon fraud alert call.

The safe way to check is simple: ignore the links in the message, open the Amazon app or type amazon.com yourself, and look at Your Orders. If you see purchases you did not make, or your sign-in no longer works, the takeover is real. If everything looks normal, the message was likely the scam, and you can delete it.

Get back in and lock the door

If you can still sign in, change your password right away to something long and unique that you have never used anywhere else. Then go to Login & Security and turn on two-step verification, ideally with an authenticator app or a passkey rather than text messages. While you are there, review the devices and sessions signed in to your account and sign out any you do not recognize, so whoever got in loses access.

If you cannot sign in because the password, email, or phone number has been changed, use the “Forgot password” flow first. If that fails, contact Amazon customer service through the help pages you reach from amazon.com directly, never from a number in a search ad or a message, and tell them plainly that your account was taken over. Ask them to lock it while they verify you.

Look for what the intruder changed

Account thieves rarely just place an order and leave. Once you are back in, walk slowly through your settings. Check the saved shipping addresses and delete any you do not recognize. Review the payment methods and remove anything unfamiliar. Look at the email address and phone number on the account, since attackers often swap these to keep control. And check Amazon Household, any linked Prime Video or Alexa devices, and your gift card balance, which is a favorite target because it can be spent quickly and is hard to trace.

It is also worth checking your messages from Amazon for anything that was sent but never reached you. Some intruders set up email rules to quietly hide order confirmations, so if your email account was involved too, our guide on what to do if your email was hacked is the next place to go. Securing your email matters even more than securing Amazon, because whoever controls your inbox can reset almost everything else.

Report the orders and ask for refunds

Cancel any unauthorized orders that have not shipped yet. For those that have, report each one to Amazon as unauthorized and request a refund. Keep notes as you go: the order numbers, the dates, who you spoke to, and what they told you. Screenshots of the unfamiliar addresses and orders are useful too, and our guide to preserving evidence explains how to save them so they hold up later.

If a charge hit a credit or debit card and Amazon has not resolved it, call your card issuer and dispute it as fraud. Do not wait too long, since dispute windows are limited. If a card number may have been exposed, ask the issuer for a replacement card. You can also file a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; it will not get your money back by itself, but it creates a record that supports your dispute.

Figure out how they got in

Most Amazon takeovers trace back to one of three things: a password reused from a site that was breached, a phishing page that looked like Amazon’s sign-in, or malware that captured saved logins. If you reused that password anywhere else, change it there too, starting with your email and banking. If you recently entered your login after clicking a link, our post on what to do after clicking a phishing link walks through the cleanup. A password manager is the single change that makes this least likely to happen again.

Over the next few weeks, keep an eye on your inbox for packages, refund notices, or password reset emails you did not trigger. Some attackers come back to an account they once controlled, and catching a second attempt early is far easier than cleaning up after it.

When it is more than a stranger

Usually, the person behind a hijacked Amazon account is an anonymous criminal who bought your login in bulk, and there is no one to identify. Sometimes, though, the pattern points closer to home: orders shipped to a nearby address, changes made right after a breakup, or the account being watched rather than used. If that sounds familiar, our guide on a partner monitoring your accounts may help, and you are welcome to reach out to us privately if you want help making sense of it.

Finding strangers inside an account you use every day is unsettling, but you have more control here than it feels like. Lock it down, report what happened, and give yourself credit for catching it.
