Student loan scam: what to do if you paid for relief

The call, text, or ad sounds like good news. A company says it has your file, that you qualify for forgiveness or a much lower monthly payment, and that it can take care of the paperwork for you. All it needs is a fee, often a few hundred dollars up front or a monthly “service” charge, along with your studentaid.gov login. A few weeks later, nothing has changed with your loans, and the company has stopped answering.

If that is where you are now, you are in good company. These operations target people who are tired, busy, and trying to do the responsible thing, and the pitch is polished enough to fool careful people. Here is how it works and what to do next.

How student loan relief scams work

The core trick is selling something that is free. Federal student loan borrowers can apply for repayment plans, consolidation, and any forgiveness program they qualify for directly through the Department of Education’s Federal Student Aid office, at no cost. A scammer charges for those same forms, or promises results that no company can promise, such as guaranteed forgiveness or a special program “only available through us.”

Common warning signs include an upfront fee, pressure to act before a deadline, a request for your FSA ID (the username and password for your federal student aid account), and instructions to stop talking to your loan servicer. Some also ask you to send your monthly payments to them instead of your servicer, which is how people end up behind on loans they thought were being handled.

What to do first

Start by securing your account. If you gave out your FSA ID, change the password at studentaid.gov right away, turn on two-step verification, and check the contact information on file. Scammers sometimes swap in their own email address or phone number so they can keep control. Do the same for any email account you used with them, and see our guide on what to do if your email account was hacked if anything looks off.

Then check the real status of your loans. Sign in on your own, by typing the address yourself, and look at your servicer, your balance, and your payment history. Contact your actual servicer and tell them what happened, so any changes the company made can be reviewed and reversed. If payments were sent to the company instead of your servicer, write down the dates and amounts.

Try to get your money back

If you paid by credit card, dispute the charge with the card issuer and explain that the service was misrepresented. Disputes have time limits, so do this soon. Debit card, bank transfer, and app payments are harder to reverse, but still call your bank, tell them it was fraud, and ask what they can do. Payments by gift card, wire, or crypto are the hardest to recover, and we cover those in separate guides, including what to do if you wired money to a scammer. Cancel any recurring charge, and watch your statements for a few months.

Report it and keep records

Save the contract or agreement, texts, emails, receipts, the company’s name and phone numbers, and screenshots of its website before it disappears. Then report to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and let Federal Student Aid know through the feedback and complaint options on studentaid.gov. Your state attorney general’s consumer protection office is also worth a call, since many states regulate debt relief companies. Reports do not always bring a quick answer, but they are how enforcement cases get built.

Watch for a second wave

Once your details are out there, expect follow-up calls. Some offer to get your money back for a fee, which is its own scam. Others use what you shared to chase you about a “debt,” so our post on fake debt collector calls and letters may help. If you gave out your Social Security number or date of birth, consider a credit freeze, which is free and fast.

Wanting help with student loans is not a mistake. Real help exists, and it is free. If you would like a second set of eyes on what happened, you are welcome to reach out to us privately.
