October 26, 2026 · Online harassment

Someone is contacting your employer to hurt you

The message might come from your manager, or from HR, or from a coworker who forwards it with a worried note. Someone has emailed your workplace, often from an address you do not recognize, with claims about you. Sometimes they are flat-out false. Sometimes they are real facts pulled out of context, or private material meant to embarrass you. Either way, the point is the same: to make your job feel unsafe.

It is a particular kind of harassment because it reaches into the one place where you cannot simply log off. If this is happening to you, it is not a reflection on you, and it is more common than most people realize. What follows is a calm order of operations for getting through it with your job and your credibility intact.

Get ahead of it, briefly and factually

If you hear about the message from someone else first, resist the urge to wait and see. People who send these emails are counting on silence, because an unanswered accusation tends to harden into whatever the reader assumes. A short, steady conversation with your manager or HR, before anyone else raises it, usually changes how the whole thing lands.

Keep it plain. Say that someone you believe is harassing you has contacted the company, that the claims are false or taken out of context, and that you are documenting everything. You do not need to prove anything in that first conversation, and you do not need to share more personal history than is useful. Ask what they received, and ask for a copy. Offer to be the point of contact so any further messages come to you as well as to them.

Ask for copies and keep the originals intact

Your employer holds the best evidence you will get: the original emails with full headers, the exact timestamps, the sending address, and sometimes attachments. Ask HR or IT to preserve the messages as received rather than forwarding screenshots, because headers can show which mail service sent them and when. If the contact was by phone, ask the person who took the call to write down what was said, the number, and the time while it is fresh.

Then build your own folder: the messages, the dates you learned about them, who you spoke to, and anything you already know about who might be behind it. Our guide on how to preserve evidence in the first 24 hours walks through a clean way to do this. Notice also whether the content points back to a source. If the sender knows details that only appear in a private account, a shared document, or an old message thread, that tells you something about where they got them and which accounts may need securing.

Check what the sender could have found about you

These campaigns usually start with a few minutes of searching. Your employer, your title, and your work email address are often on a professional profile, a company page, or a people-search site. If your workplace is easy to find from your personal accounts, consider tightening privacy settings and removing public mentions where you can. Our post on what to do if someone doxxed you covers the cleanup side, and it is worth doing even before you know who is responsible.

It is also a good moment to change passwords and turn on two-factor authentication for any account the sender might have had access to, especially if the details they used felt too specific to be a guess.

Let your employer help, within limits

Most companies would rather handle a harassing outsider quietly than lose a good employee over one. Ask whether they can route anything further from that sender to a single contact, block the address or domain, and note in your file that the messages were reviewed and no action was taken against you. A short written note confirming that matters if the person tries again later or moves to a client, a licensing board, or a professional network.

If the sender is a current or former coworker, the situation changes, and HR may be obligated to treat it as workplace harassment. In that case, put your concerns in writing and keep a copy.

Reports that tend to matter

A report to the platform or email provider can help when the messages came from a recognizable service, particularly if the account was created recently and is tied to others used against you. A police report is worth filing when there are threats, repeated contact, or any sign that someone is following your movements, and our guide on how to file a police report for online harassment explains how to make it useful. If this is part of a broader pattern of false claims, our guide to lies posted about you online covers the removal and search-result side.

We are investigators, not attorneys, and the right legal option depends heavily on where you live. An employment or defamation lawyer can often tell you in one consultation whether a demand letter is realistic and whether the messages cross a legal line.

If you need to know who is behind it

Sometimes the sender is obvious, and sometimes they are not. A name is what makes most of the serious options possible, and the technical pieces, such as email headers, account creation details, and patterns across messages, can often narrow it down more than people expect. If you would like a second set of eyes on what you have, you can reach us privately, and we will tell you plainly what is likely to be learnable and what is not.

One last thought

The goal of a message like this is to make you feel that your standing is slipping. In most workplaces it does not, particularly when you handle it calmly and early. You do not have to resolve everything this week. Talk to HR, keep the originals, and protect your accounts. Those three things do most of the work.
