Fake FBI or police agent call: what to do

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

The phone rings, and the caller says they are with the FBI, a sheriff’s office, or a federal agency. They know your name. They may say your identity was used in a crime, that a warrant exists, or that your accounts are part of an investigation, and that you must act quietly and quickly. If this happened to you, or is happening right now, take a breath first. This call is built to make a sensible person panic, and it works on careful people every day.

Here is how these calls work, what to do if you have already paid or shared something, and how to tell a real agency from an imitation.

How the law enforcement impostor scam works

The caller opens with authority: a badge number, an official-sounding office, sometimes a caller ID that really does show a government number, because phone numbers are easy to fake. They then describe a frightening problem, such as drugs found in a package under your name, money laundering through your bank account, or a missed court date. The next step is the real goal. They tell you not to hang up, not to tell anyone, and to prove your innocence by moving your money somewhere “safe,” by buying gift cards or crypto, or by handing over a code or remote access to your computer.

Some callers hand you off to a second “agent” or “prosecutor” to add weight, and some send official-looking documents with seals and case numbers. None of that changes what is happening. The pressure, the secrecy, and the demand for an unusual payment are the whole tell.

What real agencies do and do not do

The FBI and local police do not call people to demand payment, and no agency will ask you to move money to protect it, pay a fine in gift cards or crypto, or keep a call secret from your family and your bank. A real investigator is also fine with you hanging up and checking. If you want to verify, end the call, look up the agency’s main number yourself from its official website, and ask there whether the contact is real. Do not use a number the caller gave you, and do not call back the number that rang you.

If you have already paid or shared something

Speed matters, and so does staying calm. If you sent money from a bank, call the fraud department right away and ask them to recall or flag the transfer. If you bought gift cards, contact the card company with the card numbers and receipts. If you used a Bitcoin ATM or sent crypto, our guides on Bitcoin ATM deposits and wired money cover what is realistic. If you gave them remote access, disconnect the computer from the internet and see what to do if you gave a scammer remote access. If you shared a Social Security number, a password, or a code, change the passwords, turn on two-step verification, and consider a free credit freeze.

Write down everything while it is fresh: the phone number, the name and badge number they gave, the times, the amounts, and any account or wallet they pointed you to. Screenshot texts and emails. These details are what regulators use, and they are also what any later investigation would rely on.

Report it

File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and with IC3.gov, especially if money was sent. Your local police can also take a report, though be honest with yourself that these callers are often outside your area, and many victims hear little back. We wrote about that frustration in what to do when you reported a scam and no one responded.

Watch for the second call

After a loss, some victims get a follow-up from someone claiming to be an agent who can recover the money for a fee, or who says the case is being “resolved” if you pay a little more. That is the same scam returning with a kinder voice. A real agency never charges you to get your own money back.

If you are unsure whether a call was real, or you would like a calm second opinion on what to do next, you are welcome to reach out. It is private, and there is no judgment here. Being frightened by an official-sounding call does not make you foolish. It means the script did what it was designed to do.
