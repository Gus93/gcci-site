What to do if you paid a deposit in a puppy scam

You found the puppy online. The photos were perfect, the breeder answered every question, and the price felt like a stretch but a fair one. You sent a deposit to hold the litter’s pick. Then came a shipping fee, then a refundable crate fee, then an insurance fee, and somewhere in there the puppy stopped feeling real. If you are reading this with that sinking feeling, take a breath. You are not foolish for wanting to bring a dog home, and you are not the first person walked through this exact script.

A puppy scam is a fake-listing fraud. There is no dog. The whole arrangement is built to collect one deposit after another from someone whose guard is down because they are excited, not because they are careless. Knowing how it works makes the next steps clearer.

How the puppy scam works

It almost always starts with a listing or an ad — a breeder website, a classifieds post, a social media page, or a marketplace listing — with adorable photos and a price that is low enough to feel like luck. The photos are usually stolen from real breeders or pulled from older posts. Once you are interested, the seller insists on a deposit to reserve the puppy, and they will only take payment by methods that are hard to claw back: Zelle, Cash App, Venmo, Apple Pay, gift cards, a wire, or crypto.

The deposit is never the end. After you pay, the fees appear, each with a story attached. A special climate-controlled crate. Pet insurance required by the airline. A refundable shipping deposit you will supposedly get back at delivery. A customs or vaccination fee. Each one comes with urgency — the puppy is at the airport, the crate is too cold, you will lose the deposit if you do not act now. The pressure is the product. It keeps you paying before you have time to step back and look at the whole picture.

The 2026 twist: photos and video you can’t trust

It used to be that asking for a live video call would flush out a fake seller, because they had no actual dog to show. That tell is weaker now. AI image and video tools can generate a convincing puppy on demand, and some operations will send custom photos with today’s newspaper in frame or even join a short video call using generated footage. A live video is reassuring, but in 2026 it is no longer proof. The reliable checks are the boring ones: whether you can verify the breeder exists independently, meet the dog in person, and pay in a way that protects you.

If you already paid, move on the payment first

What you can do depends on how you paid, and the first hours matter most. Contact the payment provider or your bank right away, tell them it was a fraudulent transaction, and ask whether it can be reversed, recalled, or disputed. If you paid by card, you may have chargeback rights for goods never delivered. Same-day app payments can occasionally be canceled if the other side has not yet collected them. We have walked through the specific moves for several methods that come up in these cases: Zelle, Cash App, Venmo, a wire, and gift cards.

Save the evidence before the listing disappears

Fake breeder sites and seller accounts tend to vanish once a buyer pushes back, so capture everything now. Screenshot the listing, the website address, the seller’s profile, and the full conversation, including the payment requests and any photos they sent. Save the payment confirmations, the receiving name or handle, and any phone number or email they used. Keep copies in two places. Our guide to preserving evidence in the first 24 hours covers how to do this cleanly so the record holds up later.

Report it where it counts

File a report with ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, because this is an online crime, with IC3.gov. Include the listing, the seller’s details, how you paid, and the amounts. If you handed over personal information during the “adoption paperwork” — your address, date of birth, or a copy of your ID — treat that as its own issue and start a recovery plan at IdentityTheft.gov. Reporting will not always bring the money back, but it gives investigators and payment providers something concrete to act on, and it flags the listing for the next person searching for the same breed.

Spotting the next one

A few patterns hold up well. The seller wants a deposit before you can meet the dog, and they steer you toward payment methods with no buyer protection. The price is noticeably below normal for the breed. The fees keep growing after the deposit, always with urgency attached. The same photos or text show up elsewhere when you run a reverse image search. And a legitimate breeder or shelter will let you visit in person or arrange a verified meeting — a refusal to do anything but ship is the loudest signal of all. The same deposit-first pattern shows up across marketplace sales, which we cover in how to tell if a Facebook Marketplace seller is a scammer.

Losing money you set aside for a pet stings in a particular way, because the whole thing started from something good. Move on the payment, save what you have, and report it. None of that is wasted, and it is how the trail stays warm enough for someone to follow.
