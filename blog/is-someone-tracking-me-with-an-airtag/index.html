Is someone tracking you with a hidden AirTag?

Your phone put up a notice saying an item was detected moving with you. Or you heard a faint chirp from somewhere in the car and couldn’t place it. Or you found the thing itself — a white plastic disc the size of a large coin, tucked into the lining of a bag, wedged under a seat, slipped inside the spare tyre well.

Before anything else: this often has a dull explanation. A partner’s tag left in a jacket you borrowed. A rental car with a previous customer’s tracker still in the glovebox. A friend’s luggage tag riding along in a shared suitcase. The alert exists precisely so ordinary mix-ups get noticed, and most of the time that is all it is turning up.

But if the person you would least like to have your location is someone you know — an ex, a former housemate, a family member you’ve pulled away from — then the order in which you do the next few things matters more than the speed.

Think for a minute before you disable it

The instinct is to rip the battery out immediately. That is the right move if you simply want it to stop and the source is likely harmless. It is the wrong first move if someone is using it to keep tabs on you deliberately, because the moment the tag goes dark they know you found it. If you were quietly planning to leave, change your routine, or gather evidence, that knowledge can arrive at a bad time.

So there are really two decisions, and they are separate: make yourself physically safe now, and decide when to switch the tag off. If you feel unsafe, don’t drive home to sort it out — go somewhere public, or to a police station, or to someone you trust, and deal with it from there. If there is an abusive relationship in the picture, this is worth talking through with an advocate before you act; the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) can help you think about sequencing, and that call costs you nothing.

Finding the tag

On an iPhone or iPad running iOS 14.5 or later, tap the tracking notification, then tap through to Play Sound, or Find Nearby if it appears, which walks you in with distance and direction. If you dismissed the alert earlier, it hasn’t vanished: open Find My, tap Items, then Items Detected With You. Since iOS 17.5 the same alerting covers non-Apple trackers that follow the shared industry standard, so a Tile or Chipolo can surface the same way.

Android has had unwanted-tracker detection built in since Android 6.0, under safety or device-connection settings depending on the make; on much older phones, Apple’s own Tracker Detect app will scan on demand. Either way, the alerts depend on Bluetooth and location being switched on and the phone not being in airplane mode, which is worth checking if you suspect tracking but have never seen a notification.

If the sound won’t play or the distance never closes, the tag may simply not be with you any more. If you think it still is, search the places you don’t look often: seam linings, the outer pocket of a bag you rarely open, under the spare tyre, behind a licence plate, inside a child’s backpack.

What the tag can tell you

This part is quietly the most useful, and people skip it. Hold the top of your phone against the tag until a notification appears, then tap it. A web page opens showing the device’s serial number and a partial identifier for whoever registered it — the last four digits of a phone number, or a masked email address. If you know the person, those four digits are often enough to recognise them.

Screenshot that page. Then photograph the tag where you found it, before you move it, and keep the tag itself somewhere safe rather than throwing it away. The serial number is the thread investigators actually pull: law enforcement can request the registration records behind it from Apple, and without the serial there is nothing to ask about. Apple publishes its law enforcement guidelines if an officer says they don’t know the process. The full detection and disabling steps are also documented in Apple’s personal safety guide.

Note the dates and times as you go, along with where you were when each alert fired. A tag that shows up across a week of separate journeys is a very different story from one appearance after a shared taxi, and that pattern is what makes a police report or a protective order petition land.

Two honest limits

First, quiet tags exist. An AirTag only chirps once it has been away from its owner for a while, so if the person tracking you lives with you or works alongside you, it may never make a sound. Speaker-disabled units are also sold, and plenty of cheap trackers were never built to announce themselves. A silent search of your belongings and vehicle is therefore worth doing even when no alert has appeared.

Second, a tracker is rarely the only channel. When someone knows your movements, the tag is often the piece you happened to find rather than the whole arrangement — shared location in Find My or Google Maps, a family plan, an old iCloud or Google login still signed in on a device you replaced, or monitoring software on the phone in your hand. Our guide on how to tell if there is stalkerware on your phone covers that sweep, and it is the natural next step after the tag is handled.

Reporting it

Tracking another adult’s movements without consent is unlawful in most of the United States under stalking, harassment, or electronic surveillance statutes, and it is treated seriously when the report includes a serial number and a dated pattern. Take the tag, your screenshots, and your notes to local police and ask for a report number even if they seem unsure what to do with it — that number is what a protective order application, a civil attorney, or a later prosecution builds on.

If your home address is how they found you in the first place rather than the tag, the underlying leak is worth closing: our guide on removing your information from people-search sites deals with that, and if private details have already been posted publicly, what to do if someone doxxed you covers the takedown side.

The feeling afterwards

People describe the same thing after finding a tag: not fear so much as the disorientation of realising that a stretch of ordinary life — a school run, a doctor’s appointment, a drive to a friend’s house — was being watched while it felt private. That reaction is proportionate. It also passes faster once the tag is in a drawer, the screenshots are saved, and someone official has a record of it.

You noticed something most people would have walked past. That is the hard part done.
