Paid a fake invoice? What to do next

The email looks routine. An invoice arrives for a few hundred dollars: a software renewal, a domain registration, a “security plan,” or a repair you do not remember ordering. Or it comes from a company you really do pay, with a short note saying their bank details have changed and this month’s payment should go to a new account. You pay it, and later find out the bill was never real.

If that has happened to you, you are in good company. These scams work because they look like ordinary paperwork, and ordinary paperwork gets paid quickly. Here is how they work, what to do in the first hours, and what usually happens afterward.

The two versions of this scam

The first kind is an invoice for something you never bought. It usually carries a believable company name, a logo, an invoice number, and a payment link or phone number for questions. It relies on the hope that you will assume someone else in the household or office ordered it, or that you will call the number to dispute it and end up talking to the scammer. Our guide to the Geek Squad renewal email scam shows how that call-back trap plays out.

The second kind is a changed-payment-details request. A contractor, landlord, title company, or supplier you really work with appears to email you, but the message comes from a lookalike address, or from their real mailbox after it was compromised. The only thing that has changed is where the money goes. This version often involves larger amounts, and it is common in home purchases, renovations, and small-business payments.

What to do right away

If you paid by wire or bank transfer, call your bank’s fraud line now, not tomorrow, and tell them you sent money based on a fraudulent invoice. Ask them to contact the receiving bank and request a recall. Time matters a great deal here, and our guide on what to do if you wired money to a scammer walks through that call step by step. If you paid by card, call the issuer and ask about a dispute. If you paid through a payment app, contact the app and your bank at the same time.

Then stop all further payments tied to that sender, even small ones, and do not reply to the email or call any number printed on the invoice. If you are unsure whether a bill is real, contact the company using a phone number or website you find independently, or one from a past statement you already trust.

Check whether an account was compromised

If the invoice appeared to come from a real vendor, or if your own mailbox may have been used, it is worth checking for a compromise. Look for unfamiliar forwarding rules, sign-in alerts, and sent messages you did not write. Our guide on what to do if your email account was hacked covers the cleanup. It is also kind to tell the real vendor, so they can warn their other customers and secure their own systems.

Keep the evidence

Save the original email with full headers if you can, the invoice file, any payment confirmation, and the account details you sent money to. Take screenshots before anything is deleted. This material is what your bank, the police, and any investigator will ask for first, and it is much easier to keep it now than to rebuild it later.

Report it

Report the fraud to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to IC3.gov, especially if the payment was a wire or transfer. Also file a local police report if the amount was significant, because your bank may ask for a report number. If it feels like the reports go nowhere, you are not alone; we talk about that in what to do when you reported a scam and no one responded.

What recovery really looks like

Recovery is possible when the bank acts quickly and funds are still in the receiving account, but it is never guaranteed, and it gets less likely with each day. If you are contacted by anyone offering to get the money back for an upfront fee, treat it as a second scam. Real help comes from your bank, your card issuer, or the authorities. Paying a fake invoice does not mean you were careless. It means someone built a bill that looked just like the real thing, and that is their skill, not your failing.
