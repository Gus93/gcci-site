Someone filed for unemployment in my name: what to do

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

A letter arrives from your state unemployment office about a claim you never filed. Or a debit card shows up in the mail that you didn’t order. Or your employer mentions that someone applied for benefits using your name, or you spot a tax form in January listing unemployment income you never received. It is a strange, unsettling moment, because nothing was hacked on your end and you may not have done anything wrong at all.

This is a form of identity theft, and it is more common than most people expect. Someone with your name, Social Security number, and date of birth — often taken from an old data breach — files a claim and routes the money to an account or card they control. The fix is mostly paperwork, and the sooner it starts, the cleaner it tends to go.

Report it to your state unemployment agency first

Unemployment insurance is run by each state, so the claim has to be disputed with the state that paid or is processing it. The U.S. Department of Labor keeps a page on reporting unemployment identity fraud with a directory of state agencies. Use that directory, or type the state’s official website into your browser yourself, rather than clicking a link in an email or text. Fraudsters know people are worried right now, and fake “unemployment fraud help” pages exist to collect the same personal details all over again.

Most states have an online fraud form, and some ask for a police report or a signed statement. Keep a note of the date you reported, any confirmation number, and the name of anyone you spoke with. If you got a debit card or letter you didn’t ask for, hold on to the envelope and the card, and don’t activate or use it.

Tell your employer, if they contacted you

Employers are often the first to learn about a bogus claim, because the state sends them a notice about a former worker. If your employer or HR team reached out, confirm that you did not file, and ask them to respond to the state as well. Their response usually helps the state flag the claim faster. If you have no current employer connection to the claim, that is worth noting in your report, since it points to your identity being used without your knowledge.

Be careful at tax time

This part catches people off guard. When benefits are paid in your name, the state may issue a Form 1099-G showing income you never received. The Department of Labor’s guidance is to include only income you actually received when you file your taxes, and not to wait for a corrected form or for the state’s investigation to finish. If you have already filed with the fraudulent income included, don’t amend the return on your own; contact the IRS for guidance. Our guide on what to do when someone filed a tax return in your name covers the related steps, including the Identity Protection PIN.

Check your credit and close the doors

Someone who has your Social Security number for a benefits claim may have used it elsewhere. Pull your free reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and look for accounts, inquiries, or addresses you don’t recognize. If anything looks off, file a report at IdentityTheft.gov, which builds a recovery plan and gives you an official report you can hand to banks and agencies. Consider a credit freeze while you sort things out; it is free and stops new accounts from being opened in your name. Our post on what to do if someone opened accounts in your name walks through that side in more detail.

Find out how they got your information

You may never learn exactly how your details were obtained, and that is okay. A notice from an old breach is a common source, so it is worth checking whether you received one and following the steps in our guide to data breach notification letters. Changing passwords on your email and financial accounts and turning on multi-factor authentication is a sensible precaution in the meantime, since those accounts are where a second round of fraud would usually start.

People sometimes ask whether the person behind the claim can be identified. Realistically, most of these claims come from organized rings that use stolen identities in bulk, and the state and federal investigators are the ones with the authority to follow the money. Your report matters because it is one of the data points that lets them connect the pattern. A fast, well-documented report is the most useful thing you can do, and a freeze on your credit is the best protection going forward.

A claim like this is a nuisance to untangle, not a judgment on you. Report it, protect your taxes, lock down your credit, and keep your paperwork in one place. If you want a second set of eyes on where to start, we are here.
