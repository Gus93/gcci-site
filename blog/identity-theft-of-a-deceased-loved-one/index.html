Identity theft of a deceased loved one: what to do

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

You are already carrying a lot. Then a collection notice arrives in your mother’s name, or a credit card offer addressed to a husband who passed in the spring, or you learn a tax return was filed for someone who is gone. It feels like an insult on top of the loss. If this is where you are, please know it is more common than most families realize, and it is something you can sort out one step at a time.

Identity thieves look for people whose records may go unwatched for months. A person who has died is not checking their credit, and the paperwork of an estate is slow. Thieves can use a name, birth date, and Social Security number to open cards, apply for loans, or file a false tax return. Often the details came from an old data breach, an obituary that listed too much, or a stack of mail that sat in a drawer. It is rarely a sign that you did something wrong.

Start by getting the right people notified

If you have not already, make sure the Social Security Administration knows about the death. A funeral home usually reports it, but the agency also takes calls at 1-800-772-1213. Next, order several certified copies of the death certificate. You will likely need one for each bank, lender, insurer, and credit bureau, and having extras on hand saves weeks of waiting.

Then write to each of the three credit bureaus, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, enclosing a certified copy. Ask them to place a “deceased” alert on the file and to send you a copy of the credit report. Each bureau has its own process and paperwork, and the person asking is usually expected to be a spouse or the executor of the estate, so check each one’s current instructions before you mail anything. The deceased alert tells lenders to stop and verify before approving new credit, which is exactly what you want. If you want to understand how freezes and alerts differ, our guide to freezing your credit explains the basics.

Read the credit report carefully

The reports you request will show whether anything new has appeared since the death. Look for accounts you do not recognize, recent inquiries, and addresses that do not belong. If you find fraud, you are dealing with the same situation we describe in what to do if someone opened accounts in your name, with one difference: the executor or a close family member acts on the person’s behalf. Contact each lender in writing, send the death certificate and proof of your role, and explain that the account was opened after the death by someone else. Keep copies of everything you send.

Report it, even if it feels pointless

File a report at IdentityTheft.gov, the FTC’s site, which creates a recovery plan and records the fraud. A report with your local police can also be useful, since some lenders ask for one. Be ready for a mixed reception. Some departments are not sure how to handle a case where the victim has died, so bring the death certificate and your documentation, and politely ask for a report number you can give to creditors.

If a tax return was filed in the person’s name, the process runs through the IRS and is usually handled by the executor. Our post on a tax return filed in your name describes how that works, and a tax professional who handles estates can walk you through the deceased-taxpayer details.

Keep a simple record

It helps to keep one folder, paper or digital, with every letter, call date, reference number, and name of the person you spoke with. This is the same habit we describe in preserving evidence in the first 24 hours, and it matters here because estate matters can drag on and different people may take over the file. It also protects you if a creditor later insists the debt is real.

Protect what is left

A few small habits reduce the chance of a repeat. Forward the person’s mail to the executor, and shred anything with account numbers. Ask one trusted person to manage the deceased’s information and share it only as needed. Close or memorialize online accounts rather than leaving them dormant, and leave the birth date and home address out of any future obituary or memorial post. If the same thief seems to be working against other relatives, or a child in the family, it is worth reading about child identity theft too.

You do not have to untangle this alone. If a case is stuck, the fraud keeps coming back, or you suspect someone close to the family is involved, text or email us and we can talk it through privately.
