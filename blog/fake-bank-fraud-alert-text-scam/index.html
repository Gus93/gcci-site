July 3, 2026 · Impersonation scam

Is this bank fraud alert text a scam?

It starts with a text that looks exactly like the real thing. “Did you attempt a $947.20 purchase at a retailer in another state? Reply YES or NO.” You reply NO, because of course you didn’t. Within minutes your phone rings, and the caller ID shows your bank’s name. A calm, professional voice from the “fraud department” thanks you for responding so quickly and says they will help you secure your account.

That call is the trap. The fake bank fraud alert text exists for one reason: to make you pick up the phone already convinced your money is in danger, so that everything the caller asks for feels like protection instead of theft.

Why it looks so convincing

Caller ID can be spoofed, so a call really can display your bank’s name and number while coming from a scammer. The text often arrives in the same thread as genuine bank alerts, because your phone groups messages by the sender ID the scammer copied. And the caller may already know your name, your card’s last four digits, or your address, pulled from old data breaches. None of that proves you are talking to your bank. It only proves the person did their homework.

What the caller actually wants

However long the script runs, it ends in one of a few places. They ask you to read back a one-time passcode that was just texted to you, which is them logging into your account in real time and using you to defeat your own security. They ask you to “reverse” the fraudulent charge by sending money to yourself through Zelle or a wire, which routes it to an account they control. Or they tell you your money must be moved to a “safe account” while they investigate. A real bank will never ask you to move money to protect it, never ask you to read out a security code, and never ask for your PIN or full online banking password. Ever.

If you are on the call right now

Hang up. You do not owe a polite exit to someone who called you. Then, if you want to check whether anything is actually wrong, call the number printed on the back of your card or shown inside your banking app, and say a caller claimed to be from the fraud department. Do not call any number from the text, and do not trust a follow-up call back, because scammers often redial after a hang-up to say “we got disconnected.”

If you already gave a code or moved money

Act now rather than perfectly. Call your bank on the official number, tell them what happened, and ask them to secure the account and attempt a recall of any transfer. Change your online banking password from a device the caller never touched, and turn on the strongest login protection your bank offers. If money left through Zelle, our guide on what to do after sending money to a scammer on Zelle walks through the recovery steps, and if it left by wire, start with what to do if you wired money to a scammer. Before you delete anything, capture the text, the phone numbers, and the timeline, the way we describe in preserving evidence in the first 24 hours.

Report it, even if you caught it in time

File a report with ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov, including the sender number, the caller’s number, and any account details they used. If you shared your Social Security number or full banking credentials, also work through the recovery checklist at IdentityTheft.gov. You can forward the scam text to 7726 (SPAM), which helps carriers block the sender. Reports rarely feel satisfying in the moment, but they are how these operations get mapped and, eventually, shut down.

One more thing worth knowing

This scam has a bigger sibling. If the caller escalates from a suspicious charge to a story about hackers inside your accounts and a government official who will “help” you move everything somewhere safe, you are looking at the phantom hacker scam, and the same rule applies: your bank will never ask you to move your money. If you have already lost a significant amount and need help documenting what happened, our Investigation Help page explains the options.

Getting a text like this does not mean your account was hacked. It usually means your phone number is on a list. Slowing down for one phone call, the one you place yourself to the number on your card, is the whole defense.
