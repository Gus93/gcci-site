Reshipping scam: what to do if you forwarded packages

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

The job sounded almost too easy. A company you had never heard of offered remote work as a “package handler,” “parcel inspector,” or “logistics assistant,” and all you had to do was accept deliveries at your home, check them, and send them on to a new address with a label they provided. Maybe a few paychecks even arrived. Now a carrier has asked questions, a bank has flagged something, or the whole arrangement has started to feel off. That feeling deserves a closer look, and you are not the first person to land here.

What you were doing is called reshipping, and it is a well-known scam. The FTC has warned about it repeatedly, and it tends to climb around the holidays, when a flood of packages makes one more box easy to hide.

Why it’s a scam, and who it is built to use

The goods that arrive at your door are usually bought with stolen credit cards or stolen identities. Sending them through a stranger’s address, and then onward, hides where they came from and makes the trail harder to follow. You were never really an employee. You were a middleman whose home address and name sit between the thieves and the investigators, which is the same role a money mule plays with cash. Our guide on what to do if a scammer used you as a money mule covers that side of it.

The warning signs are often easy to see afterward. The “employer” hired you with no real interview, often over text or chat. The pay was high for the work, or never arrived. You may have been asked to cover postage out of your own pocket, or to pay with a check you were told to deposit, which is a fake check problem of its own. And the destination addresses were overseas or kept changing. Many of these offers begin the way the fake job offer that starts with a text does.

Stop shipping, and keep what you have

The first step is simple: stop accepting and forwarding packages. Tell the “employer” nothing about why, and do not argue with them. If packages are still arriving, do not open or discard them, and do not ship them onward. Keep the boxes, the shipping labels, the tracking numbers, and the instructions you were given, because those are the evidence that you were deceived rather than involved on purpose.

Then save everything about the arrangement before it disappears. Take screenshots of the job posting, the messages, the emails, any contract or onboarding form, the payment records, and the names and numbers used. Chat accounts and job listings vanish quickly once a scammer senses you are pulling back, so do this first and think about the rest after.

Report it, including the packages

File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and with IC3.gov, and include the carrier names and tracking numbers if you have them. Because the scheme runs through the mail, the U.S. Postal Inspection Service is also a useful place to report if USPS handled any of the packages. If you still hold packages, tell your local police what happened and ask how they want the items handled. Calling them yourself, before anyone contacts you, shows plainly that you are a person who was misled. I am not a lawyer, and I cannot tell you how your particular situation will be treated, but if you shipped a large number of packages or received any notice from a carrier, retailer, or investigator, speaking with an attorney is a sensible step.

Protect your identity and your money

Think about what you handed over. Jobs like this usually collect your full name, home address, date of birth, and often a Social Security number or bank details for “payroll.” If you gave any of that, put a freeze on your credit (our walkthrough on how to freeze your credit takes a few minutes), watch your bank statements, and change the passwords on any account the “employer” had a login for. If you paid for postage or supplies, ask your bank or card issuer whether the charges can be disputed, and tell them why.

Be wary of anyone who contacts you afterward offering to clear your name or get your money back for a fee. That is a second scam on top of the first, and a real refund or a real answer will not come from a stranger who wants payment first.

What can be traced

It is worth being realistic about attribution. The people behind reshipping operations usually work overseas behind throwaway email accounts, spoofed company names, and rotating addresses, and naming an individual is rare. What helps is the pattern: reused company names, shipping addresses, and message wording that tie your report to many others. That is why reporting, even when no money was lost, matters more than it seems.

You were offered a job and treated like a real employee, which is exactly how this works. Stopping, keeping the evidence, and reporting it is the right response, and it will help the next person who gets the same offer.
