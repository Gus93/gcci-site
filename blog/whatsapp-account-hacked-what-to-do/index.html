WhatsApp account hacked? What to do right now

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

A friend texts to say you just sent them something strange. Or you open WhatsApp and find you have been logged out, or a code you never requested lands on your phone. Then the messages start coming in from people in your contacts: someone, using your name and photo, has been asking them for money, a favor, or a code. Having your messaging account taken over feels invasive, and it is also very common. It is fixable, and the first hour matters most.

Here is how these takeovers usually work, how to get back in, and how to protect the people who trust your messages.

How someone gets into your account

Most takeovers are not technical break-ins. The attacker registers your phone number on a new device, which makes the app send you a six-digit verification code. They then need you to hand that code over, and they get it with a believable story: a friend’s account that was “locked,” a code “sent to you by mistake,” a contest entry, a job verification. Often the message comes from a contact who was already hijacked, which is why you trusted it. The moment you share the code, the account is theirs. We cover the same trick on another service in our post on what to do if you read back a Google verification code.

Less often, the attacker takes your number itself through a SIM swap or by having your calls forwarded, so the code goes straight to them. If your phone suddenly lost service, read what to do if your phone has no service first, because that changes the order of what you do.

Take the account back

Install the app if needed, enter your own phone number, and request a new verification code. When you finish registering, the app moves to your device and the attacker is logged out, because an account can only be active on one phone at a time. If a six-digit code arrives, never share it with anyone, even someone you know. If the attacker turned on two-step verification and you are asked for a PIN you did not set, you may be locked out for a period while the app waits to let you reset it. When you are, use the in-app option to contact support and say your account was stolen, from the app itself so it comes with your number.

Once you are back in, open the linked devices list in settings and log out of anything you do not recognize, then turn on two-step verification with a PIN only you know and add a recovery email. Those two steps are what stop the same trick from working again. If you use Telegram, Signal, or another app, the idea is similar: end any sessions you do not recognize and turn on the registration lock or two-step setting.

Warn your contacts, calmly

This is the part people skip, and it matters most. Send a short note to your contacts, or post it in a group chat, saying your account was hijacked, that nothing sent in the last day or so came from you, and that they should ignore any request for money, codes, or links. A person who already sent money, or gave up a code of their own, is exactly where our guide on how to help someone who is being scammed can help. If any contact sent money to the attacker, they should treat it like any payment scam and contact their bank or payment app right away.

Check the rest of your digital life

If you reuse a password, or the attacker had access to your messages, assume your other accounts deserve a look. Change the passwords on email and social media, turn on authenticator-based two-step login where you can, and check your phone carrier account for forwarding or SIM changes you did not make. Do the email account first, since resets for everything else flow through it. Our post on what to do if your social media account was hacked has the longer checklist.

Report it, and save what you can

Report the takeover to the app’s support team, then file a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, if money moved, with IC3.gov. Before you clear anything, screenshot the strange messages your contacts received, the code request, and the timeline of when you were logged out. Those details help if a bank, platform, or investigator needs to trace what happened.

Being taken over by a message you trusted says very little about you. The scheme works because it comes through people you already believe, so cleaning it up and warning those people is the right thing to do.
