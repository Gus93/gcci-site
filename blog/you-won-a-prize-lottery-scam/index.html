Is that “you won a prize” message a scam?

A message arrives that feels like good news for once. You won a lottery you don’t remember entering, a sweepstakes tied to a familiar brand, a gift card giveaway, or a foreign lottery that names a real jackpot from the headlines. The person sounds official, congratulates you warmly, and then explains that before the winnings can be released you just need to cover a small cost first. A processing fee. A tax. Insurance on the transfer. Customs. Once that is paid, they say, the money is yours.

If you are reading this because that last part gave you pause, your instinct is a good one. Wanting to believe a piece of luck is not naive, and the people running these schemes design them to arrive exactly when a bit of extra money would be welcome. Here is how to tell what you are looking at, and what to do if you already sent something.

The one thing every prize scam has in common

Strip away the branding and the excitement and every version of this scam comes down to a single move: to collect your winnings, you have to pay first. That is the tell, and it is close to a law of nature. A real lottery, sweepstakes, or giveaway never asks winners to send money to receive a prize. Legitimate taxes on winnings are handled by you and the relevant tax authority after you have the money, not paid up front to a stranger who contacted you. The moment a “win” requires a fee, a card, a wire, or a crypto payment to unlock, it is a scam, no matter how convincing the rest of it sounds.

Once you have paid the first fee, the story usually grows. A new fee appears, then another, each framed as the very last step. That escalation is the scam working as intended, not a sign you are close to a payout.

How to tell a real notice from a fake one

A few patterns show up again and again. You are told you won a contest you never entered, or a foreign lottery you have no connection to. You are asked to keep the win confidential until it is “finalized,” which is really about keeping you away from anyone who might talk you out of it. You are rushed, with a deadline to claim before the prize is forfeited. The message has odd grammar, a personal email address rather than a company domain, or a check arrives that you are told to deposit and then send a portion back. And the payment they want is always one that is hard to reverse, like a gift card, wire transfer, payment app, or cryptocurrency.

If a check came with the notice, treat it with particular caution. The bank may show the funds as available within a day or two, but that does not mean the check is good. When it bounces weeks later, any money you sent back is gone and you are responsible for the shortfall. We walk through exactly how that works in our guide on what to do if you deposited a fake check.

What to do if you already paid

Stop sending money now, even if you have already paid once and feel invested in seeing it through. Nothing you send will release a prize that does not exist, and every additional payment is a fresh loss. After that, what is recoverable depends on how you paid. If you used a credit or debit card, call the issuer, explain you paid a fraudulent prize fee, and ask about disputing the charge. If you paid another way, act quickly, because time matters more with those methods. Our guides on what to do if you sent gift cards to a scammer and what to do if you wired money to a scammer cover the specific steps for each. Gather everything as you go: the messages, names, phone numbers, email addresses, payment receipts, and any check images. That record is what makes a report useful later.

Report it, even for a small amount

File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, which tracks prize, lottery, and sweepstakes fraud and shares data with state regulators who act on patterns across many small reports. If the pitch came by phone, mail, or a scam website, add a report at IC3.gov, the FBI’s complaint center. Fake prize notices that arrive by mail can also be reported to the U.S. Postal Inspection Service, since using the mail this way is a federal offense. Your report matters even if the amount felt small; it is often the small ones, added together, that let investigators see the shape of a whole operation.

Watch for the follow-up

People who lose money to a prize scam are frequently contacted again, sometimes by someone claiming they can recover the lost funds for an upfront fee, sometimes by the original scammer under a new name offering a “bigger” prize to make up for the trouble. Both are the same scam a second time. Real refunds come from your bank, your card issuer, or a regulator, never from a stranger who reaches out first and asks to be paid before they help. Our piece on telling a real recovery process from a recovery scam covers the red flags to watch for.

Hoping a bit of good fortune is real is a very human thing, and the people behind these schemes count on it. Pausing to check, refusing to pay a fee to claim a prize, and reporting what happened is exactly the right response, and it is worth telling the people around you about it too.
