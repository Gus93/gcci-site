What to do if you fell for a sugar daddy scam

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

It usually starts with a message out of nowhere. Someone on Instagram, X, Snapchat, or a dating app says they are wealthy, busy, and looking to “spoil” someone kind — no meetups required, just a weekly allowance for your time. The offer sounds strange but harmless. Then the money is supposed to arrive, and something small gets in the way: a fee to “verify” you, a test of your loyalty, a check that needs to be deposited and partly sent on. If you are reading this, you may have already paid, deposited something, or shared more than you meant to.

A sugar daddy scam is one of the most common money scams aimed at young adults, and it works on smart, careful people every day. Feeling foolish is normal. It is also not useful right now. What matters is what you sent, what you received, and what you shared, because each of those has its own next step.

How a sugar daddy scam usually works

There are a few versions, and most follow the same arc. In the first, the “sugar daddy” (or sugar mommy) promises a large first payment but says their bank, their accountant, or the payment app needs you to send a small amount first to unlock it. The fee grows each time you pay it. In the second, they send you money — a mobile check deposit, a bank transfer, or a payment-app transfer — and ask you to send part of it to someone else, often framed as paying their “assistant” or proving you can be trusted. The original payment later bounces or is reversed, and the money you sent on is gone from your own account.

In the third version, they ask for your online banking login so they can “deposit directly.” That gives them a clean account to move stolen money through, and it can leave you answering questions from your bank about transactions you never made. And sometimes the conversation turns to photos, with the person later threatening to share what you sent unless you pay. Whichever version you hit, the promised allowance was never coming.

If you sent money

Contact the bank, card issuer, or app you paid through as soon as you can, tell them plainly that you were scammed, and ask what can be stopped or disputed. Speed matters most with peer-to-peer apps and gift cards, where a window of hours can make a difference. Our guide on sending money on Cash App to a scammer walks through those specific steps. Do not send anything more, even if the next message promises the full allowance is finally ready.

If they sent you money first

This is the version that catches people off guard, because the money really did show up in your account — at first. Banks often make deposited funds available before a check or transfer has fully cleared, and when it fails days later, the bank takes the full amount back from you, including whatever you already forwarded. If you have deposited a check or received a transfer from this person and have not sent anything on, stop there and call your bank to tell them what happened. If you already passed money along, tell your bank that too. Our post on what to do if you deposited a fake check covers how banks handle this and what to ask for.

If you gave out your banking login, or your account was used to receive and move money for them, change your password and turn on two-factor authentication right away, then call the bank’s fraud line. Being honest early protects you. People in this situation are usually treated as victims, not participants, especially when they come forward on their own. Our piece on what to do if a scammer used you as a money mule explains why and how to handle that conversation.

If you shared photos or personal details

If the conversation has turned into threats to share images, stop replying, do not pay, and save the messages and the account names before you block. Paying rarely ends the demands. If you are under 18, or the photos were taken when you were, Take It Down can help keep those images from spreading on participating platforms; adults can use StopNCII.org for the same purpose. Both work by creating a digital fingerprint of the image on your own device, so you never have to upload the photo itself.

If you shared your address, your real name, or where you work or study, it is worth tightening your social media privacy settings and keeping an eye on your accounts for the next few weeks. Most of these operators are working from a script and move on quickly once a target stops responding.

Report it, and keep what you have

Before you delete anything, take screenshots of the profile, the chat, any payment requests, and the usernames or payment handles they used. Then report the account to the platform where it contacted you, and file a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI’s IC3. Reports like these rarely bring a quick refund on their own, but your bank may ask whether you filed one, and they help investigators link many small cases into one bigger one.

Be wary of anyone who appears afterward offering to recover your money or delete your photos for a fee. That is a common second scam aimed at people who just reported the first.

You are not the only one

These scams are run at scale, often by groups sending the same opening message to thousands of accounts a day. They are built to feel flattering and low-risk until the moment money moves. Recognizing it now and taking a few calm steps is exactly the right response. If you are unsure which of these applies to you, or the situation has become more complicated than a single payment, reach out and we can help you sort out what to do first.
