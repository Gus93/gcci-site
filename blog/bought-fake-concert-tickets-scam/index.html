What to do if you bought fake concert tickets

Maybe the transfer link never arrived. Maybe the QR code showed up looking real, and the scanner at the gate turned red because someone else had already used it. Either way, you paid for fake concert tickets, the seller has gone quiet, and the show is going on without you. Here is what is actually worth doing now.

First, the part that is easy to forget while you are angry at yourself: ticket scammers work sold-out shows on purpose. They know you are searching under time pressure, they price just under what desperation would pay, and they often message you from a hacked account that belongs to a real person with years of normal posts. This trap is built by professionals. Falling into it is not a character flaw.

Start with the payment, today

How you paid decides what can still be undone, and the clock matters more than anything else on this page.

If you paid by credit card, or by debit card run as credit, call the card issuer and dispute the charge as goods not received or counterfeit goods. Card disputes exist for exactly this, and they succeed regularly. If you paid through a marketplace with buyer protection, like the goods-and-services option on PayPal, open the claim inside the platform first and let it run.

If you paid by instant transfer, the odds are tougher but not zero. Report the payment as a scam inside the app anyway, because banks are refunding more imposter-scam payments than they used to, and a report you never file is a refund you never get. We have step-by-step guides if you paid on Zelle, Venmo, or Cash App.

Save the evidence before the seller deletes it

Scam listings and profiles tend to vanish within hours of a buyer pushing back, so capture everything now, before you confront anyone. Screenshot the listing, the seller’s profile, the full conversation, the payment receipt, and the ticket or QR code they sent you. Save the seller’s username, phone number, and the exact payment handle the money went to. Those identifiers are what an investigator or a bank can actually pivot on later. Our guide to preserving evidence in the first 24 hours walks through how to capture it cleanly.

Report the seller in the right places

Report the account on the platform where you found the tickets, whether that was Facebook Marketplace, Instagram, X, or a resale site. If the seller was a friend’s account acting strangely, tell your friend by phone or text, because their account is likely hacked and yours may be the message that gets it recovered.

Then file with ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, since the seller took your money over the internet, with the FBI at IC3.gov. Include the payment handle, usernames, and screenshots. A single report rarely triggers action on its own, but reports are how payment accounts that collect from dozens of victims get flagged and frozen.

Expect the second scam

If you post publicly about being scammed, replies will appear recommending a “recovery agent” or “refund specialist” who can get your money back for a fee. That is the same scam coming around for a second pass, and sometimes it is the same crew. Nobody legitimate finds victims through comment sections, guarantees a refund, or asks to be paid upfront in crypto or gift cards.

Buying tickets safely next time

For future shows, buy from the venue, the artist’s official seller, or a resale platform that holds the money until the ticket transfers and verifies the barcode. If a stranger insists on Zelle, Venmo, or a wire because “the app’s transfer system is down,” that sentence is the scam. And a real mobile ticket transfers through the ticketing app itself, not as a screenshot, because a screenshot can be sold to twenty people at once.

If the loss is large

Most fake ticket losses are painful but small. If yours was not, perhaps season tickets, a group buy for friends, or a package deal worth thousands, the payment trail can sometimes be traced to a real account holder. Our Investigation Help page explains what we look at and when a paid trace is worth it, and our guide on finding a scammer’s identity gives an honest picture of what is possible.

The money may or may not come back. What you can control today is the dispute, the evidence, and the reports, and those are worth an hour of a bad day.
