Government grant scam: what to do if you paid a fee

The message often comes from someone you know. A friend on Facebook or Instagram writes that she just received a government grant, no repayment needed, and that you qualify too. She gives you the name of an “agent” to contact. The agent is friendly and professional, confirms your eligibility within a few minutes, and says your grant of several thousand dollars is approved. All that remains is a small fee to release it: a processing charge, an insurance bond, a tax, or a “verification deposit.”

If you have already sent that fee, or are about to, this guide is for you. Wanting help with bills, a business, or a house repair is a reasonable thing to want, and these schemes are built around exactly that. Here is how the scam works, what is and is not recoverable, and the steps worth taking today.

Why a real grant never asks you to pay

Government agencies do not contact people out of the blue to hand out grant money, and they never ask recipients to pay a fee, buy gift cards, or send crypto to receive one. Real federal grants are listed publicly on Grants.gov, and they go to organizations and projects such as research institutions, nonprofits, and local governments, with a formal application and review. There is no program that quietly sends personal cash grants to people who reply to a direct message. If someone says you were “selected,” you did not apply, and money is required first, you are looking at an advance-fee scam.

As with most advance-fee schemes, the first fee is rarely the last. After it is paid, a second charge appears, such as a “release code,” a tax clearance, or a fee to correct a supposed error, each presented as the final hurdle. The delay is not a sign the payout is close. It is the scam continuing.

Why it can look so convincing

Most victims are not careless. The pitch usually reaches them through a trusted face, because the sender is often a friend whose account was taken over. If that sounds familiar, our guide on what to do if your social media account was hacked explains how that happens and how to help the friend. The “agency” may also use official-sounding names, government-style logos, a fake ID number, or a website that looks professional. Official-looking is easy to fake. Asking for payment is the part that gives it away.

What to do if you already paid

Stop sending money, even if you have paid once and feel committed. Then act on how the payment was made. If you used a credit or debit card, call the issuer, say you paid a fraudulent grant fee, and ask about a dispute. If you used a payment app or bank transfer, contact your bank or the app right away; our guides on sending money on Zelle to a scammer and sending gift cards to a scammer cover the specific steps. Speed matters most with these methods, so do not wait until you feel sure.

While you do that, keep a record: screenshots of the conversation, the profile or account that contacted you, any names, phone numbers, or links, and receipts for what you paid. Do not delete the chat, even though it may feel unpleasant to keep.

Protect the rest of your information

Grant scammers often ask for a Social Security number, bank details, or a copy of your ID “to process the application.” If you shared any of that, it is worth acting on early. Placing a free credit freeze is a good first step, and we outline it in our guide on how to freeze your credit. Watch your accounts for unfamiliar activity, and be careful with the next unexpected message, because people who have paid once are often targeted again.

Report it

Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and to IC3.gov if you lost money online. Also report the profile that contacted you to the platform it was on. Reports can feel like they disappear, which is something we talk about in what to do when you reported a scam and no one responded, but they are how patterns get connected and how investigators see an operation instead of a single loss.

If someone later contacts you offering to recover your grant fee for another payment, that is the same scam again. Real refunds come from your bank, your card issuer, or a regulator, not from a stranger who reaches out first and asks to be paid. Being tempted by help is human, and pausing to check, as you are doing now, is the right move. It is also worth letting the friend whose account was used know, so they can secure it.
