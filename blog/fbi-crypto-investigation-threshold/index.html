Why didn’t the FBI investigate my crypto scam?

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

You did the right thing. You filed with IC3, got an automated confirmation and maybe a case number, and then the silence started. Weeks. Months. No call, no update, no sign anyone has opened your file. The question that follows is fair, and almost nobody answers it honestly: why isn’t the FBI working my case?

It is not that your report was wrong, or that the people at the FBI don’t care. It is that federal law enforcement runs on a resource math nobody explained to you, and for small individual cases that math is brutal.

The threshold no one will put in writing

The official line is that federal agencies take every report seriously. The operational reality is that any single cybercrime case competes for finite investigator hours against hundreds of thousands of others filed the same year. The Internet Crime Complaint Center takes in roughly 800,000 complaints annually, with total reported losses well over $12 billion, and the agency has a few hundred dedicated cyber agents to work them.

What that produces is an implicit case-selection that loosely tracks dollar value. Individual losses below roughly $50,000 rarely get an active investigation. Below $100,000 often won’t either, unless the report slots into a larger pattern the agency is already assembling. And most crypto fraud runs through overseas actors, which adds a second ceiling: even when attribution succeeds, there is frequently no realistic path to prosecution.

None of this is stated policy, and there is no published number. But ask a federal prosecutor or an FBI agent off the record and you’ll hear the same thing: the hours are scarce, and the cases that get worked are the ones where the math justifies them.

Report anyway — here is what it still does

Even when your case is below the threshold, the report is not wasted:

  • Pattern aggregation. Your case may not be investigated alone, but it joins a database the FBI uses to build cases against larger operations. A scammer who hit you for $4,000 may have hit 200 others for the same amount.
  • Statute of limitations. A timely report preserves your option to seek civil recovery later, or to join a class action if one materializes.
  • Insurance, tax loss, and protective filings. Many of these require an IC3 or police report as documentation.
  • Future protection. Reports inform consumer alerts that protect the people who would otherwise become the next victim.

So file. Always. Even when you suspect nothing will come of it directly.

The honest limit on recovery — ours and everyone’s

This is the hardest paragraph to write, because it is the one most people in your position don’t want to read. So plainly:

Once cryptocurrency has moved on-chain through a mixer, an offshore exchange, or a wallet operated overseas, no one — not GCCI, not Rexxfield, not the FBI, not any private blockchain-forensics firm — can guarantee its recovery. This is true regardless of how much you lost.

A $5,000 loss is hard to recover. A $500,000 loss is also hard to recover — the resources scale up a little, but the underlying difficulty doesn’t change.

Here is the distinction reputable investigators lead with, before you pay them anything. Tracing and recovery are not the same thing. They can attribute the wallet, trace the funds, and document the chain of custody, and that work has real value — it is what a lawyer or a court needs. But the deliverable is a written report, not a return of your money, and a completed trace does not mean a refund is coming. They will quote you a flat fee or hourly rate for that investigation work and be honest about the odds. Anyone who instead promises the money itself, or wants a percentage of it, is either dishonest or is the scam.

The recovery scam: the follow-up to plan for

Somewhere between a few weeks and a few months after the loss, someone will contact you claiming they can recover your funds. They will sound professional. They may have a website. They may even know specific details about your case — because the people who took your money sold your contact information to their next-stage operation, or they are the same crew running a second act.

The tells are consistent:

  • The first contact is unsolicited — a cold call, text, email, Telegram message, DM, or a "comment from a satisfied customer" on a YouTube video about scam recovery
  • They claim insider access — to the FBI, to blockchain forensics, to "blockchain regulators," to a specific exchange’s security team
  • They show you a dashboard or "tracking system" indicating your funds are in a recoverable state
  • They ask for an upfront fee, a "deposit," a "tax clearance payment," or your wallet credentials
  • The fee scales with whatever you initially lost — they have already priced you

If that matches what landed in your inbox: the person contacting you is part of the same operation, or a parallel one working the same victim lists. Assume so until they prove otherwise — which they cannot, because legitimate investigators do not approach victims unsolicited.

The recovery paths that are actually real

There are four, and only four:

  • Your bank or card issuer — if a chargeback window is still open and the funds left through a card or ACH transaction, they can sometimes reverse it. Fastest in the first 24–72 hours.
  • A licensed attorney pursuing civil action against an identifiable defendant. The civil burden of proof is lower than criminal.
  • Federal law enforcement — rare, threshold-dependent, never charges a fee.
  • A reputable private investigator engaged through transparent public channels you reached out to first, with a written scope and an honest disclaimer about recovery odds.

If a recovery channel charges you up front for a service whose only promised product is “your money back” — and the fee vanishes along with the work — walk away. Real investigations don’t run on contingency for fraud cases. Recovery scammers always do.

Where this leaves you

The system is imperfect, and the honest answer is uncomfortable: crypto losses to overseas actors are very difficult to recover, and federal law enforcement is not built to chase them all. But reporting still matters, attribution is sometimes possible, and none of this is a verdict on you — the system is simply operating inside its own resource limits. The people who promise to fix that for you, especially the ones who find you first, are the next chapter of the same scam.
