Financial sextortion of teens: what parents can do

Your teenager was chatting with someone who seemed friendly, close to their age, and interested in them. It moved quickly. An intimate photo got sent, and within minutes the tone changed. Now there are threats: pay money or gift cards right now, or the image goes to their friends, their team, their followers, their family. If you are reading this because your child just told you, or because you found the messages yourself, take a breath. You are already doing the most important thing, which is treating this as a problem to solve rather than a secret to keep.

This is a scam with a name. Financial sextortion is one of the fastest-growing crimes against young people, and it is run by organized groups who do this to thousands of kids at a time. The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children received more than 50,000 reports of it in 2025, averaging well over a hundred a day. Your child is not the first, and the people behind it are following a script they have used many times before.

The thing your teen most needs to hear

Before anything technical, the message that matters most is this: they are not in trouble, and this is not their fault. These offenders are practiced adults who pose as peers and manufacture trust on purpose. A young person who was manipulated into this did not do something shameful, they were targeted by someone whose full-time job is manipulation. Kids in this situation often feel there is no way out, and that fear is exactly what the blackmailer is counting on. Hearing clearly and early that they are loved and not being punished is what makes it possible for them to let you help.

Do not pay, and stop responding

Paying almost never ends it. Once money moves, the offender learns the threats are working and comes back for more, often escalating. The stronger move is to stop replying entirely. Do not send money, do not send gift cards, and do not send another image. Do not delete the account or block the person just yet, because the messages themselves are evidence and cutting contact silently is different from destroying the record. Simply stop the conversation and step back from it.

Save the evidence before anything gets deleted

The instinct to make it all disappear is completely understandable, but a few minutes of preserving first will help every step that follows. Screenshot the profile, the username, the messages, the threats, and anything that shows where the money was demanded, and note the platform and any payment app or wallet involved. If your teen already blocked the account, that is fine, but try to capture whatever remains. Our guide on how to preserve evidence in the first 24 hours walks through what to capture and what not to touch, so the record stays useful for the platform and for law enforcement.

Get the images taken down

If the person in the image is under 18, use Take It Down, a free service from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children. It creates a digital fingerprint of the image on your own device, without the image ever leaving it, and shares that fingerprint with participating platforms so they can detect and remove copies. It works even if you only fear an image might be shared. For anyone 18 or over, the equivalent tool is StopNCII.org, which we cover in our post on what to do when a scammer threatens to share your photos.

Report it

Report to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children through the CyberTipline, or by calling 1-800-THE-LOST. NCMEC works these cases specifically and can support removal and next steps. Report to the FBI as well, online at tips.fbi.gov or through IC3.gov, or by calling 1-800-CALL-FBI. And report the account directly on the platform where it happened, since most now have a dedicated flow for sextortion and image abuse. If gift cards were sent before you found out, our guide on what to do if you sent gift cards to a scammer covers the fast steps that can sometimes freeze the balance.

Look after the person, not just the case

The reports and takedowns matter, but the young person in front of you matters more. This kind of pressure can hit teenagers very hard, and the shame and fear can feel enormous in the moment. Keep checking in, keep the door open, and make sure they know none of this changes how you see them. If your child is showing signs of real distress or you are worried about their safety, you can reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline any time by calling or texting 988, and NCMEC can also connect families with support.

Sextortion works by convincing a young person they are alone and out of options. The moment an adult they trust steps in, most of that power is gone. You have already done the hardest part by not letting it stay a secret.
