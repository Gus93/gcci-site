Timeshare exit scam: what to do if you paid a fee

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

Someone called, mailed, or ran an ad promising to get you out of a timeshare you no longer want, or to sell it fast to a waiting buyer. You paid a fee, maybe a few thousand dollars, and then the weeks turned into months. The calls stopped getting returned, the “buyer” never closed, or every update came with a new reason to send more. If that sounds familiar, you are in a very common spot, and it is not a sign you were careless. People who are stuck with a timeshare they cannot afford or use are exactly who these companies look for.

The good news is that a few calm steps now can still help, especially if you paid recently. Here is where to start.

How these scams usually work

A timeshare exit scam tends to arrive in one of a few shapes. In the first, a company promises a guaranteed, hassle-free exit, collects a large fee up front, does little or nothing, and then stalls until it disappears or reopens under a new name. In the second, a “broker” says a cash buyer is ready to purchase your unit, but you must first pay taxes, wire charges, or escrow setup. There is no buyer, and those costs are the whole point. A third version uses lawyers: someone claims to be recruiting owners for a lawsuit against the developer, or says an attorney can finish the sale, and asks for a retainer. Each time you ask for a refund, the answer is often another payment to unlock the first one.

Real exits and real resales are slow and often disappointing. Anyone who guarantees a fast result, charges before doing any work, or contacts you out of the blue is worth treating with real suspicion.

Stop paying and write down what happened

The first step is to stop sending money, even if the next payment is described as the last one needed. Then gather everything while it is still easy to find: contracts, emails, texts, call logs, receipts, bank or card statements, the company’s website, and the names and numbers of everyone you spoke with. Take screenshots of ads or pages before they change. Our guide on how to preserve evidence in the first 24 hours covers the details, and a short written timeline of who said what and when will be useful to every agency and bank you talk to next.

Try to get the payment back

How you paid decides what is possible. If you used a credit card, call the issuer, explain that you paid for a service that was never delivered, and ask about a dispute. Time limits apply, so do this soon. If you paid by debit card, check, or wire, call your bank right away and ask whether the transfer can be recalled or flagged; our post on what to do if you wired money to a scammer walks through that conversation. Recovery is far from guaranteed, particularly for wires, but the first days matter most, and asking costs you nothing.

Report it, even if it feels like a long shot

File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, which tracks timeshare resale and exit fraud. Also report to the FBI’s IC3 if the contact happened online, and to your state attorney general’s consumer protection office, which is often the agency that actually pursues these companies. A Better Business Bureau complaint adds to the public record other owners will search. If you worry nothing will come of it, our piece on what to do when you reported a scam and no one responded explains why reports still matter and what else you can do.

Expect the second scam

Lists of people who already lost money get passed around. Some weeks after a timeshare fraud, victims often hear from someone posing as a government agency, a law firm, or a “recovery” company that can get the money back, or finally complete the exit, for a processing fee. That is the same scam wearing a different badge. Real refunds come from your bank or card issuer, or from an agency or court, and not from a stranger who contacted you first and wants payment up front. We lay out the same pattern in how to tell a real recovery process from a recovery scam.

What about the timeshare itself?

The timeshare is still yours, along with its maintenance fees and contract terms. Before you stop paying anything to the resort or developer, speak with a licensed attorney in your state or contact the developer directly about its own exit or deed-back program, since missed fees can lead to collection or foreclosure. GCCI is not a law firm and this is not legal advice, but a quick consultation here is worth the cost, and anyone telling you to simply stop paying and stop talking to a lawyer is a red flag.

Wanting out of a financial burden is a normal thing to want. Being talked into a bad deal by people who do this for a living says a lot about them and nothing about your judgment. Keep your records, report what happened, and if you would like help organizing your evidence, you are welcome to text or email us.
