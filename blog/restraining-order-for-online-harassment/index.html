Restraining order for online harassment: how it works

When someone will not stop contacting you online, at some point the question changes from “how do I block them” to “is there anything a court can do?” It is a reasonable question, and the answer is often yes. A restraining order, which many states call a protective order or an order of protection, is a court order telling a specific person to stop contacting you. Harassment that happens entirely online can qualify, although the rules differ a good deal from one state to the next.

This is general information, not legal advice, and we are not a law firm. What follows is meant to help you walk into a courthouse or a lawyer’s office knowing what to expect.

Does online harassment count?

In most places, yes, though it depends on what is happening and who is doing it. Many states have laws against stalking and cyber-harassment that cover repeated messages, threats, posting about you, creating accounts to contact you after you blocked them, or monitoring you through technology. Some orders are available to anyone being stalked or harassed, while others are written mainly for people with a family, household, or dating relationship to the other person. If you do not have that kind of relationship, ask the court clerk or an advocate whether your state offers a stalking or harassment order, because that is often the route that fits.

Courts also tend to care about pattern and impact. One nasty message may not be enough, but repeated contact after you asked for it to stop, especially with threats or fear attached, usually is. Do not talk yourself out of asking just because it “is only online.”

What evidence helps

The strongest petitions show a clear timeline: what was sent or posted, from which account, on what date, and that you told the person to stop. Screenshots that include the username, the date and time, and the full conversation are far better than cropped images. Keep the original messages too, rather than only the screenshots, and write down in a simple log how each incident affected you. Our guide on how to preserve evidence in the first 24 hours explains how to capture it in a way that holds up. If you have already filed a police report, bring the report number, and if you have not, our guide on how to file a police report for online harassment covers what to say.

How the process usually works

You generally start at the courthouse, either with the clerk’s office or a self-help center, and fill out a petition describing what has happened. Filing is often free or very low cost for protective orders. In many places a judge can review the petition the same day and issue a temporary order while a full hearing is scheduled, sometimes without the other person being present for that first step. The person then has to be formally served with the papers, and at the hearing both sides can present evidence. If the judge grants it, the order usually lasts for a set period and can often be renewed.

Local domestic violence and victim advocacy programs can often help with the paperwork and go with you to court at no charge. WomensLaw.org has state-by-state information on how to file, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 can connect you with local help, including when the person harassing you is not a partner.

The part people worry about: not knowing who it is

An order needs a named person, so anonymous harassment is harder. If you do not know who is behind the account, that is usually a problem to solve first, and sometimes a court case is part of how it gets solved, since lawyers can ask a court to compel a platform to identify an account holder. Our guide on what to do when someone keeps making new accounts to harass you covers documenting a pattern when the accounts keep changing. If you think you know who it is but cannot prove it, say so honestly to the clerk or your lawyer rather than guessing in the petition.

If they violate the order

An order only helps if it is enforced. If the person contacts you again, including from a new account or through a friend, save it exactly as you did before and report it to the police, because violating the order is often a crime in its own right. Keep a copy of the order with you or on your phone. Online platforms will also generally act faster on reports when you can point to a court order.

Think about your safety, not just the paperwork

Orders are useful, but they do not physically stop anyone, and the period right after one is served can sometimes be when things escalate. If you fear for your physical safety, talk with an advocate about a safety plan, tighten the privacy on your accounts, and consider who in your life should know what is going on. If anything feels immediate, contact your local emergency number first and sort out the court process afterward.

You do not have to decide today whether to file. Start by saving what you have and talking with a local advocate or attorney about what your state allows.
