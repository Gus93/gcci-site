Fake online store scam: what to do if you ordered

By Lisa · Founder & Lead Investigator

The shop looked right. The photos were crisp, the price was a little better than everywhere else, and checkout took a minute. Then the tracking number went nowhere, or the package that finally arrived held a cheap knockoff, or nothing came at all and the support email now bounces. That sinking feeling is common this time of year, when shoppers hunt for deals and scammers set up storefronts to meet them.

You did not do anything unusual. Fake stores are built to look like ordinary shopping, and some are polished enough to fool people who check carefully. Here is how to work out what happened and what to do next, starting with the steps that are time-sensitive.

Why these stores are hard to spot

A fake online store is usually a template site stood up in a day or two, stocked with product photos copied from real retailers. Many are promoted through social media ads, often showing a brand-name item at a steep discount or a “closing down sale.” Some copy a real retailer’s name with one letter changed or a different ending, like .shop or .store. Others take your money and send something nothing like the listing, which can make it feel like a bad seller instead of a scam.

Common tells include a domain registered only weeks ago, no verifiable business address or phone number, reviews that are all five stars and oddly similar, and payment options limited to things that are hard to reverse. A price that is far below every other seller is the most reliable warning, though not the only one.

Start with the payment, and do it quickly

What you can recover depends on how you paid, and timing matters, so begin here before anything else. If you used a credit card, call the number on the back of the card and ask to dispute the charge as an item not received or not as described. Credit card disputes generally carry the strongest protections, and a card issuer can also replace the card, which you will want anyway. If you used a debit card, call the bank right away and say the same thing; protections are weaker than for credit cards, but a prompt report helps.

If you paid through PayPal, open a dispute in its Resolution Center before the dispute window closes. If you used a payment app or sent money as a personal payment, the options are narrower, but report it to the app immediately. Our guides on sending money through PayPal to a scammer and wiring money to a scammer cover the specific steps. Gift cards and crypto are the hardest to pull back, though it is still worth reporting them quickly.

Protect the card and the details you entered

Even if the dispute goes smoothly, the store now has your name, address, phone number, and card details. Ask your card issuer to close the card and issue a new one, then watch statements closely for small test charges in the weeks ahead. If you reused a password to create an account on the site, change it everywhere you use it, and turn on two-step verification for your email. If you clicked an ad or link to reach the store and entered anything else, our post on what to do after clicking a phishing link is a good checklist. If you gave more than payment details, such as a date of birth or ID number, consider a free credit freeze as well.

Keep what you have, then report it

Before the site vanishes, save what you can: screenshots of the product page, your order confirmation, the receipt email, tracking numbers, the store’s URL, and any messages with “support.” Note the ad or post that led you there, since reporting the ad is often the quickest way to get it taken down. Your card issuer may also ask for these.

Then report it. File with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and if you lost money, add a report at IC3.gov. Report the ad to the social media platform it ran on, and the site to its hosting company or domain registrar if you can find them through a public lookup. Small reports matter here, because fake stores are usually caught through patterns across many complaints, not through one large loss. If a package did arrive but it was something you never ordered, that is a different pattern, which we cover in our piece on unordered packages and brushing scams.

Beware the “recovery” follow-up

Once you have reported the store, expect that someone may contact you offering to get the money back for an upfront fee. That is a second scam, and a common one. Real recovery comes from your card issuer, your payment service, or a regulator, never from a stranger who reaches out first and wants payment before anything happens.

Checking a store before the next purchase

Searching the store’s name along with the word “scam” or “reviews” takes half a minute and catches a lot. Look up when the domain was registered with a free lookup tool, since a brand-new site selling an established-looking inventory is a red flag. Type the retailer’s address yourself instead of tapping an ad, and use a credit card or a payment service with buyer protection for anything from a seller you have not used before. A deal that seems too good is worth a second look, even when the site is attractive.

If you are not sure what happened, or the amount was large, you can reach us any time. Nothing about this means you were careless. It means someone put real effort into looking trustworthy.
